## Key ideas

### The EU as a supranational body

The European Union, established in its current form by the 1992 Maastricht Treaty, pools sovereignty: member states share decision-making in areas like trade, competition and the environment, and EU law can override national law in those fields. Its institutions — the Commission, the Council and the directly elected Parliament — blend supranational and intergovernmental elements. Supporters see pooled sovereignty as amplifying small states' influence; critics, including supporters of the UK's 2016 vote to leave, argue it dilutes democratic control at the national level.

### The United Nations: promise and limits

Founded in 1945 after the Second World War, the UN aims to prevent another global conflict through diplomacy, peacekeeping and international law. The General Assembly gives every member state one vote but its resolutions are mostly non-binding, while the Security Council can authorise sanctions or military action — yet any of the five permanent members can veto such moves, which is why the Council often deadlocks over major conflicts. The UN's record is therefore mixed: a vital forum and aid coordinator, but constrained whenever great powers disagree.

**Key term — supranational:** A supranational body holds authority above its member states in specific areas, so its rules can bind national governments — the EU is the leading example.

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **'The UN can force any country to obey it'** The UN has no army of its own and its most powerful body, the Security Council, can be blocked by a single permanent member's veto. Its authority depends on member states' cooperation.
  - **'Globalisation has ended sovereignty'** Globalisation constrains sovereignty — states cannot fully control capital flows, data or pollution — but states still choose treaties, set borders and leave organisations, as Brexit showed. Sovereignty is pressured, not abolished.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Name the five permanent members of the Security Council and explain the veto." hint="Think of the major post-war powers.">
    The US, UK, France, Russia and China; each can veto Security Council decisions, blocking action even if all others agree.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="State two requirements of jus ad bellum." hint="Think about cause and last resort.">
    Just cause and last resort (also acceptable: legitimate authority, proportionality of ends, reasonable chance of success).
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Explain the security dilemma." hint="Defence that looks like attack.">
    When one state's defensive military build-up frightens neighbours into arming too, escalating tension even though no side wanted war.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="When was the UN founded, and for what main purpose?" hint="1945, after the war.">
    In 1945, to maintain international peace and prevent another world war.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
