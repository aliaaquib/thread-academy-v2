## Global Challenges

A virus doesn’t check passports. CO₂ doesn’t respect borders. The problems states most need to solve together are the ones cooperation finds hardest.

<Definition term="Collective action problem">
  A **collective action problem** arises when everyone benefits from cooperation but each actor gains by free-riding — so rational self-interest produces collective failure, like overfishing a shared sea.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Climate change: the ultimate collective action problem — emitters benefit, everyone pays.
- Free-riding: why cut emissions if others won’t? Treaties try to solve this.
- Pandemics showed both cooperation (vaccines in a year) and failure (vaccine hoarding).
- Global governance is weak but real: the UN, WHO, Paris Agreement.

<Example title="The ozone success story">
  The 1987 Montreal Protocol phased out CFCs — every country complied, and the ozone hole is healing. Proof that global cooperation can work when science is clear and substitutes exist.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Why is climate change harder to solve than ozone depletion?" hint="Substitutes and scope.">
    CFCs had cheap substitutes and few producers; fossil fuels power whole economies with no easy substitute, and every country is involved.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is free-riding in global politics?" hint="Benefits without costs.">
    Enjoying the benefits of others’ cooperation (e.g. a stable climate) without bearing the costs yourself.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Borderless problems need cooperation anarchy resists.
  - Free-riding is the core obstacle.
  - The ozone treaty proves cooperation can succeed.
  - Global governance exists — but remains fragile.
</Summary>
