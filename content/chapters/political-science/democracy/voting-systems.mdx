## Voting Systems

In 2015, UKIP won 12.6% of UK votes — and 1 seat out of 650. The system, not just the voters, decided that.

<Definition term="Proportional representation">
  **Proportional representation (PR)** awards seats in proportion to votes won. Contrast first-past-the-post (FPTP), where each district elects one winner — simple, but often wildly disproportional.
</Definition>

## Key points

- FPTP: one winner per district; tends to two big parties, stable single-party government.
- PR: seats match votes; fairer, but often coalitions.
- No system is neutral: rules shape party numbers and strategies (Duverger’s law).
- ‘Fairest’ depends on values: proportionality vs stable government vs local representation.

<Example title="2015: 12.6% of votes, 0.15% of seats">
  Under FPTP, UKIP’s evenly spread support won almost nothing; the SNP’s concentrated Scottish support won 56 of 59 seats with 4.7% of UK votes. Geography plus rules decided it.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Why does FPTP tend to produce two-party systems?" hint="Wasted votes.">
    Voters avoid ‘wasting’ votes on small parties, and small parties can’t win districts — so support consolidates into two big parties (Duverger’s law).
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Give one argument for and one against PR." hint="Fairness vs decisiveness.">
    For: seats match votes — fairer representation. Against: frequent coalitions can mean slower, less decisive government.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Voting rules translate votes into power — non-neutrally.
  - FPTP favours big parties and stable government.
  - PR is fairer but usually means coalitions.
  - Choose the system by the values you prioritise.
</Summary>
