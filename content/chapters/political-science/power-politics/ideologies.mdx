## Political Ideologies

‘Left’ and ‘right’ began as seating positions in revolutionary France. The labels stuck — but the ideas underneath matter more.

<Definition term="Ideology">
  An **ideology** is a coherent set of ideas about how society works and should work — covering human nature, the economy, the state and change. It tells followers what’s wrong and what to do.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Liberalism: individual freedom, rights, markets with rules.
- Conservatism: tradition, order, scepticism of rapid change.
- Socialism: equality, collective/common ownership, state provision.
- Beyond: feminism, environmentalism, nationalism, populism — and the libertarian–authoritarian axis.

<Example title="One policy, four ideologies">
  Free school meals: liberals see equal opportunity; socialists see the state correcting inequality; conservatives worry about dependency and cost; libertarians ask why the state’s involved at all.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="How do liberals and socialists differ on the state’s economic role?" hint="Referee vs player.">
    Liberals want the state to set fair market rules; socialists want it to own or direct key industries and redistribute more deeply.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why is left–right alone an incomplete map?" hint="Two axes.">
    It misses the libertarian–authoritarian dimension: a left-libertarian and left-authoritarian agree on economics but not on state power.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Ideologies package views of human nature, economy and state.
  - Liberalism, conservatism and socialism are the classic three.
  - Left–right needs a second axis: liberty vs authority.
  - Ideologies frame — but don’t settle — policy debates.
</Summary>
