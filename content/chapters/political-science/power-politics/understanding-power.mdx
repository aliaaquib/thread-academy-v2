## Key ideas

### Weber on power, Nye on soft power

Weber's definition reminds us that power is relational: it exists between people, and resistance is part of the picture. Building on this, the American scholar Joseph Nye distinguished hard power — coercion through military force or economic pressure — from soft power, the ability to attract rather than force, through culture, values and diplomacy. A country that others admire and wish to imitate wields soft power; Nye later called the skilful combination of both 'smart power'.

### Weber's three types of legitimate authority

Weber argued that authority is legitimate when people believe in its rightness, and he identified three pure types. Traditional authority rests on long-standing custom, like a hereditary monarchy. Charismatic authority rests on the exceptional personal qualities of a leader, such as a revolutionary figure or movement founder. Rational-legal authority rests on impersonal rules and offices, like an elected president or a civil servant whose power comes from the law, not the person. Most modern states blend all three, but rational-legal authority dominates liberal democracies.

**Key term — power:** Max Weber defined power as the chance of carrying out your own will even when others resist. In plain terms, it is the ability to get your way despite opposition.

<WorkedExample title="Analysing a Protest Scenario">
  Imagine a government bans a peaceful march against a new law. One minister says the ban shows the state's strength; a journalist says it shows the state's weakness. Using Weber and Nye, who has the better argument?

  Step 1: Identify the power being used. Banning the march is hard power — coercion through police and law.
  Step 2: Test for legitimacy. Peaceful protest is normally protected in a liberal democracy, so the ban may look like authority losing its legitimate basis and sliding into mere force.
  Step 3: Consider soft power. A confident government persuades; reaching for bans suggests its soft power — attraction and persuasion — is failing.
  Step 4: Weigh both views. The ban demonstrates the state still holds the monopoly of force, but using it against peaceful dissent usually signals political weakness, not strength.

  **Answer:** The journalist has the stronger argument. The ban proves the state retains Weber's monopoly of force, but deploying hard power against peaceful protest suggests failing legitimacy and failing soft power — a sign of weakness, not strength.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Confusing power with authority** Power is the ability to get your way; authority is power people accept as rightful. A bully has power, but a fairly elected leader has authority. Examiners reward answers that keep the distinction sharp.
  - **Treating Weber's three types as boxes that never mix** Weber called them 'ideal types' — analytical tools, not descriptions of pure reality. A modern prime minister mainly holds rational-legal authority but may also draw on charisma, and exam answers should discuss the blend.
</ImportantNote>
