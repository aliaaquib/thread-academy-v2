## Key ideas

### Can we speak meaningfully of God?

The logical positivists challenged religious language directly: A. J. Ayer's verification principle declared statements meaningful only if verifiable by sense experience, making 'God exists' meaningless. Antony Flew extended the attack with falsification - if nothing could count against a religious claim, it asserts nothing (his parable of the invisible gardener). Responses include Aquinas's analogy, R. B. Braithwaite's view of religious language as moral commitment, Paul Tillich's theory of symbols, and Wittgenstein's language games - each trying to show religious speech has its own legitimate logic.

### The coherence of the divine attributes

Classical theism holds that God is omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good and eternal - but each attribute raises puzzles. Can an omnipotent God create a stone too heavy to lift? Aquinas replied that omnipotence means power over all that is logically possible, and a self-contradictory 'unliftable stone' describes nothing at all. Can God know the future without destroying human freedom? Boethius argued that God's eternity means God sees all time at once, as present, rather than foreseeing it. Theology proceeds by refining definitions, not abandoning them.

**Key term — analogy:** Aquinas's theory that religious language is neither univocal (exactly the same meaning) nor equivocal (completely different meaning) but analogical - partly similar to ordinary use.

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **If religious claims cannot be verified, they are meaningless** That is the verificationists' claim, not a settled result - it was itself challenged as self-refuting (the verification principle cannot be verified) and by alternative accounts of religious language.
  - **Omnipotence means God can do absolutely anything, including the logically impossible** Classical theologians from Aquinas onwards define omnipotence as power to do all that is logically possible; contradictions describe nothing and are not 'things' to be done.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Explain Aquinas's doctrine of analogy in one sentence." hint="Between same and different.">
    Words like 'good' applied to God are used analogically - neither with exactly the same meaning as in human contexts (univocal) nor with a wholly unrelated meaning (equivocal), but with a meaning partly similar and partly greater.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="State Ayer's verification principle." hint="Meaningful only if...">
    A statement is meaningful only if it is analytic (true by definition) or empirically verifiable; otherwise it is meaningless.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="How does Aquinas define omnipotence?" hint="Logically possible.">
    The power to do all that is logically possible - not the power to do what is self-contradictory.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What did the Council of Nicaea (325 CE) define?" hint="Three persons.">
    The doctrine of the Trinity: God is one being in three persons - Father, Son and Holy Spirit.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
