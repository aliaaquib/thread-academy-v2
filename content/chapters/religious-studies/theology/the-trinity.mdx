## Key ideas

### The Trinity: unity and distinction

The doctrine of the Trinity, defined at the Council of Nicaea in 325 CE, holds that God is one being in three persons - Father, Son and Holy Spirit. It is not tritheism (three gods) and not modalism (one God wearing three masks): the persons are really distinct yet fully share the one divine nature. Theologians call this a mystery - not a puzzle to be solved, but a truth to be explored - and use analogies (such as Augustine's mind, with memory, understanding and will) while admitting every analogy breaks down.

### The coherence of the divine attributes

Classical theism holds that God is omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good and eternal - but each attribute raises puzzles. Can an omnipotent God create a stone too heavy to lift? Aquinas replied that omnipotence means power over all that is logically possible, and a self-contradictory 'unliftable stone' describes nothing at all. Can God know the future without destroying human freedom? Boethius argued that God's eternity means God sees all time at once, as present, rather than foreseeing it. Theology proceeds by refining definitions, not abandoning them.

**Key term — omnipotence:** All-powerfulness; classically understood as the power to do anything that is logically possible.

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **The Trinity means Christians believe in three gods** The doctrine affirms one God in three persons - tritheism is explicitly rejected as heresy; the persons share one divine nature.
  - **Omnipotence means God can do absolutely anything, including the logically impossible** Classical theologians from Aquinas onwards define omnipotence as power to do all that is logically possible; contradictions describe nothing and are not 'things' to be done.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="How does Aquinas define omnipotence?" hint="Logically possible.">
    The power to do all that is logically possible - not the power to do what is self-contradictory.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Distinguish general from special revelation." hint="Nature versus scripture.">
    General revelation is God known through nature, reason and conscience; special revelation is God known through specific acts such as scripture, prophets or Christ.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="State Ayer's verification principle." hint="Meaningful only if...">
    A statement is meaningful only if it is analytic (true by definition) or empirically verifiable; otherwise it is meaningless.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is Flew's falsification challenge?" hint="The invisible gardener.">
    If no possible evidence could count against a religious claim, then the claim asserts nothing - illustrated by the parable of the invisible gardener.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
