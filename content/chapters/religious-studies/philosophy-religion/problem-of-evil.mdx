## The Problem of Evil

Earthquakes kill children. Diseases ravage the innocent. If God is all-good and all-powerful, why doesn’t He stop it? This is philosophy of religion’s hardest question.

<Definition term="Theodicy">
  A **theodicy** is an attempt to justify God’s goodness despite evil’s existence — e.g. free will requires the possibility of wrongdoing; soul-making needs real challenges; natural laws have byproducts.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Logical problem: the three claims (omnipotent, wholly good, evil exists) seem contradictory.
- Evidential problem: even if compatible, the AMOUNT of suffering counts against God.
- Free-will defence: genuine love and goodness require free choice — which requires the possibility of evil.
- Soul-making (Hick): virtues like courage need real danger; natural evil as the price of a law-governed world.

<Example title="The fawn in the forest fire">
  A fawn burns, unseen, in agony. No human learns from it; no soul is ‘made’. Pointless-seeming suffering like this is the evidential problem’s sharpest case.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Distinguish the logical and evidential problems of evil." hint="Contradiction vs weight of evidence.">
    Logical: the claims are logically incompatible. Evidential: they’re compatible but the scale of suffering makes God improbable.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="How does the free-will defence answer the problem?" hint="Freedom needs the option of wrong.">
    God values free creatures; freedom logically requires the genuine possibility of choosing evil — a world of free beings can’t be risk-free.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Evil is the strongest challenge to classical theism.
  - Theodicies include free will, soul-making and natural law.
  - Gratuitous suffering remains the hardest case.
  - The debate turns on what ‘all-powerful’ and ‘good’ mean.
</Summary>
