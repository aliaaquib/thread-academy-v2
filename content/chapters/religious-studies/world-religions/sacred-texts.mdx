## Sacred Texts

A text doesn’t interpret itself. The same verses have justified both liberation and oppression — so HOW scriptures are read matters enormously.

<Definition term="Hermeneutics">
  **Hermeneutics** is the theory of interpretation — the principles by which readers draw meaning from texts. In religion it decides whether a verse is read literally, metaphorically, historically or mystically.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Bible (Christianity), Qur’an (Islam), Torah/Tanakh (Judaism), Vedas/Upanishads (Hinduism), Guru Granth Sahib (Sikhism), Pali Canon (Buddhism).
- Literalism: the text means what it says. Contextualism: meaning depends on historical context.
- Scripture in practice: recitation, memorisation, liturgy, law, meditation.
- Translation always interprets — which is why some traditions privilege the original language.

<Example title="Qur’anic memorisation">
  Millions of Muslims memorise the Qur’an in Arabic — huffaz — preserving it orally as well as in writing, in an unbroken chain from the 7th century.
</Example>

## Practice

    Texts are read through hermeneutic lenses — literal, historical, metaphorical — producing very different meanings and moral conclusions.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Give one reason some traditions emphasise the original language of scripture." hint="Translation = interpretation.">
    Translation inevitably interprets; the original is seen as preserving the revelation exactly as given.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Every major tradition centres sacred texts.
  - Hermeneutics — how you read — shapes what texts mean.
  - Scripture lives in practice: recitation, law, worship.
  - The same text can sustain many readings.
</Summary>
