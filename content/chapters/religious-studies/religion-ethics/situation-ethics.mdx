## Key ideas

### Fletcher's situation ethics and virtue ethics

Joseph Fletcher's situation ethics (1966) keeps one absolute - agape, selfless love - and treats all other rules as guidelines to be set aside when love demands it, making it relativistic and teleological. Critics object that agape is too vague to guide decisions and that the theory can justify anything. Virtue ethics takes a different path: following Aristotle, it asks what kind of person one should become, cultivating virtues such as honesty, courage and compassion. Alasdair MacIntyre revived this tradition, and it fits naturally with religious emphases on character and spiritual formation.

### Aquinas and natural law

Thomas Aquinas argued that God built purpose (telos) into human nature, and that reason can discover the moral law by reflecting on it. The primary precepts - preserve life, reproduce, educate children, live in society, worship God - are known to all rational beings, while secondary precepts apply them to particular cases. Natural law is absolutist and deontological: an act that violates a primary precept, such as murder, is always wrong regardless of consequences. Critics ask whose account of 'human nature' counts, and whether the theory smuggles Catholic conclusions into supposedly universal reason.

**Key term — agape:** Selfless, unconditional love; for Joseph Fletcher the only absolute rule in ethics.

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Situation ethics means anything goes** Situation ethics has one absolute - agape love - and requires careful calculation of which action produces the most loving outcome; it is principled, not lawless.
  - **Natural law just means following your instincts** Natural law means using reason to discern God's purpose (telos) in human nature - it often restrains instinct rather than following it.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Give one criticism of situation ethics." hint="Think about vagueness.">
    Any of: agape is too vague to guide hard decisions; it can justify breaking any rule; outcomes are hard to predict, so loving calculation may fail.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="How does virtue ethics differ from rule-based theories?" hint="Character versus acts.">
    Virtue ethics focuses on the kind of person one should become and the virtues to cultivate, rather than on rules for judging individual acts.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Is natural law absolutist or relativist? Why?" hint="Rules without exceptions?">
    Absolutist: acts that violate the primary precepts are always wrong, regardless of circumstances or consequences.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="State the two horns of the Euthyphro dilemma." hint="Because it is good, or good because commanded?">
    Either God commands what is good because it is good (making goodness independent of God), or things are good because God commands them (making morality seem arbitrary).
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
