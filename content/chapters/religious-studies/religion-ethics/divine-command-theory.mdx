## Key ideas

### The Euthyphro dilemma

Plato's dialogue Euthyphro poses a famous challenge to divine command theory. If God commands things because they are good, then goodness is independent of God and God is unnecessary to morality. But if things are good simply because God commands them, then morality seems arbitrary - God could have commanded cruelty, and 'God is good' becomes the empty claim 'God does what God commands'. Modern defenders such as Robert Adams respond that God's commands flow from God's necessarily loving nature, so they are neither arbitrary nor independent of God.

### Aquinas and natural law

Thomas Aquinas argued that God built purpose (telos) into human nature, and that reason can discover the moral law by reflecting on it. The primary precepts - preserve life, reproduce, educate children, live in society, worship God - are known to all rational beings, while secondary precepts apply them to particular cases. Natural law is absolutist and deontological: an act that violates a primary precept, such as murder, is always wrong regardless of consequences. Critics ask whose account of 'human nature' counts, and whether the theory smuggles Catholic conclusions into supposedly universal reason.

**Key term — Euthyphro dilemma:** Plato's question: does God command what is good because it is good, or is it good because God commands it?

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Divine Command Theory">
  State the two horns of the Euthyphro dilemma.

  Either God commands what is good because it is good (making goodness independent of God), or things are good because God commands them (making morality seem arbitrary).

  **Answer:** Either God commands what is good because it is good (making goodness independent of God), or things are good because God commands them (making morality seem arbitrary).
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **The Euthyphro dilemma has no answer** It is a powerful objection, but philosophers have proposed replies - such as Adams's claim that God's commands express God's necessarily good nature - which defenders and critics continue to debate.
  - **Natural law just means following your instincts** Natural law means using reason to discern God's purpose (telos) in human nature - it often restrains instinct rather than following it.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="How does virtue ethics differ from rule-based theories?" hint="Character versus acts.">
    Virtue ethics focuses on the kind of person one should become and the virtues to cultivate, rather than on rules for judging individual acts.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Name two of Aquinas's primary precepts of natural law." hint="Preserve life...">
    Any two of: preserve life, reproduce, educate children, live in an ordered society, worship God.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Is natural law absolutist or relativist? Why?" hint="Rules without exceptions?">
    Absolutist: acts that violate the primary precepts are always wrong, regardless of circumstances or consequences.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is Fletcher's single absolute in situation ethics?" hint="A Greek word for love.">
    Agape - selfless, unconditional love; every decision must aim at the most loving outcome.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
