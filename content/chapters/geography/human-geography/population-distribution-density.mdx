Human geography is the geography of people: where we live, why we move and how we connect. In this chapter you will explain uneven population distribution, apply the push–pull model to migration, account for urban growth, and trace the global connections that tie economies together.

This lesson focuses on Population Distribution: explain why people cluster in some places and avoid others.

  Explain why people cluster in some places and avoid others.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Distribution is uneven for good reasons

People cluster where conditions suit them: fertile plains, navigable rivers, coasts and mineral resources attract settlement, while deserts, high mountains and frozen lands stay sparsely populated. In the UK, the south-east is densely settled while the Scottish Highlands are sparse — relief, climate and economic history all play a part.

### Migration is driven by push and pull factors

Push factors drive people away — unemployment, low wages, poor services, conflict or natural disasters. Pull factors attract them — jobs, higher wages, better schools and healthcare, or family already there. Most migration is economic, and most migrants move from rural areas to cities within their own country.

**Key term — population density:** The average number of people per square kilometre — high density means crowded, low density means sparse.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Population Distribution">
  Define population density.

  The average number of people per square kilometre.

  **Answer:** The average number of people per square kilometre.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Confusing population density with total population** A country can have a huge population but low density if it is vast, like Canada. Density is people per square kilometre, not the total headcount.
  - **Mixing up urbanisation and urban growth** Urban growth is cities getting bigger in absolute terms; urbanisation is the rising proportion of people living in urban areas. A country's cities can grow without it urbanising if the rural population grows too.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Is fleeing a flood a push or a pull factor?" hint="Does it drive people away or attract them?">
    A push factor — it forces people to leave.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Give one positive and one negative effect of rural–urban migration on the countryside left behind." hint="Think about who leaves and what they send back.">
    Positive: fewer people competing for jobs; remittances sent home. Negative: loss of young, able workers — a 'brain drain' from rural areas.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Give two physical reasons why an area might be sparsely populated." hint="Think about land that is hard to live on.">
    Any two: mountainous relief, desert climate, extreme cold, dense forest, poor soils.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Name the two main causes of urban growth." hint="One is movement, the other is births minus deaths.">
    Rural–urban migration and natural increase.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
