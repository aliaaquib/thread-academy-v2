## Genders and Cases

Yes, German nouns have genders — der Mann, die Frau, das Kind — and ‘the’ changes with the noun’s job in the sentence. It’s systematic, not random.

<Definition term="Case">
  A **case** marks a noun’s role: nominative = the subject (der Mann), accusative = the direct object (den Mann). The article changes to show the role — der becomes den for masculine objects.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Three genders: masculine (der), feminine (die), neuter (das). Learn every noun WITH its article.
- Nominative (subject): der/die/das. Accusative (direct object): den/die/das — only masculine changes.
- Tricks: -ung/-heit/-keit are feminine; -chen/-lein are neuter; most -er nouns masculine.
- Plural article is always ‘die’ (nominative/accusative).

<Example title="The dog bites the man — unambiguously">
  ‘Der Hund beißt den Mann’ — even if you reorder the words, den marks the man as the victim. Cases make word order flexible.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="In ‘Ich sehe den Hund’, why is it ‘den’ not ‘der’?" hint="What’s the dog’s role?">
    ‘Hund’ is masculine and the direct object (accusative), so der → den.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What’s the golden rule for learning German nouns?" hint="Never alone.">
    Always learn the article with the noun — not ‘Tisch’ but ‘der Tisch’.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Three genders: der/die/das — learn articles with nouns.
  - Accusative marks direct objects; masculine der→den.
  - Endings hint at gender (-ung feminine, -chen neuter).
  - Plural ‘the’ is always die.
</Summary>
