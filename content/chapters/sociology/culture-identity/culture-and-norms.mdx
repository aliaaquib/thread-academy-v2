## Culture and Norms

Culture is the software societies run on: shared meanings, rules and expectations that make behaviour predictable enough for strangers to cooperate.

<Definition term="Norm">
  A **norm** is an expected pattern of behaviour in a given situation — from folkways (etiquette) to mores (moral rules) to laws (formalised, enforced rules). Sanctions — rewards and punishments — keep norms alive.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Values: broad ideals (freedom, fairness). Norms: specific expected behaviours.
- Folkways: customs (covering your mouth when you cough). Mores: moral weight (taboos). Laws: written and enforced.
- Subcultures share the wider culture but differ in parts; countercultures reject it.
- Cultural relativism: judge practices by their own culture’s standards — while still debating universal rights.

<Example title="The queue">
  Britain’s queue is a folkway with teeth: jump it and face collective fury. No law requires it — just shared norms and social sanctions (glares, tuts, confrontation).
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Distinguish a folkway from a more." hint="Etiquette vs morality.">
    Folkways are everyday customs with mild sanctions (table manners); mores carry moral significance and stronger sanctions (theft, assault).
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is cultural relativism, and what’s one criticism of it?" hint="Whose standards?">
    Judging practices by their own culture’s standards rather than yours. Criticism: taken absolutely, it can’t condemn human-rights abuses anywhere.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Culture = shared meanings, norms and values.
  - Norms range from folkways to mores to laws.
  - Sanctions enforce norms informally and formally.
  - Subcultures differ within; countercultures oppose.
</Summary>
