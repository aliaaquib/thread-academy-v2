## Research Methods

How do you study something as big as a society? Carefully, and from several angles at once.

<Definition term="Representative sample">
  A **representative sample** mirrors the population being studied — so findings can be generalised. Random sampling gives everyone an equal chance of selection; biased samples produce misleading results.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Surveys/questionnaires: large samples, quantitative — but shallow and prone to bias.
- Interviews: rich qualitative detail — but small samples, hard to generalise.
- Participant observation: deep insider understanding — but time-consuming and subjective.
- Official statistics: huge datasets — but collected for government purposes, not research.

<Example title="Studying a subculture">
  To understand football hooligans, sociologists joined them for seasons — participant observation revealing codes of honour no survey would catch.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Why might a survey of ‘voters’ conducted outside one train station mislead?" hint="Who uses that station?">
    It’s an unrepresentative sample — commuters at one station don’t mirror the electorate’s age, class or views.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Give one strength and one weakness of interviews." hint="Depth vs breadth.">
    Strength: rich, detailed insight into meanings and motives. Weakness: small samples, interviewer effects, hard to generalise.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - No single method captures society — triangulation helps.
  - Sampling determines whether findings generalise.
  - Quantitative methods count; qualitative methods understand.
  - Every method has trade-offs.
</Summary>
