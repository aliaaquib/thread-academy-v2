This lesson focuses on Spotting Bias: how to detect one-sided sources without dismissing them.

<Definition term="Spotting Bias">
  How to detect one-sided sources without dismissing them.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Bias is a clue, not just a flaw

A biased source is not automatically useless. A suffragette newspaper is biased towards votes for women — but that bias tells you exactly what suffragettes thought and felt. The mistake is treating a one-sided source as the whole truth.

### Strong answers weigh sources

In exams, top answers do more than describe sources: they compare them, note agreements and disagreements, and judge usefulness using provenance. Saying 'Source A is useful because it is a primary source' is weak; saying 'Source A is useful for showing X, but limited because...' is strong.

**Key term — Bias:** A one-sided view in a source, favouring one side. A biased source can still be useful if you understand its slant.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Spotting Bias">
  Give one reason a source might be biased.

  It was made to persuade people to one side, e.g. propaganda or a campaign leaflet.

  **Answer:** It was made to persuade people to one side, e.g. propaganda or a campaign leaflet.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Writing 'the source is biased so it is useless'** Bias does not make a source useless. Explain what the bias reveals and what the source cannot show, instead of dismissing it.
  - **Calling every old source a primary source** A primary source must come from the time studied. A 1990s documentary about the Victorians is secondary, however old it looks.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="What is the difference between a primary and a secondary source?" hint="Think about when each was made.">
    A primary source comes from the time studied; a secondary source is made later, using primary sources.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="A suffragette newspaper supports votes for women. Is it useless to a historian?" hint="Bias can be a clue.">
    No — it is excellent evidence of what suffragettes believed and how they argued, though one-sided on the wider debate.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What does provenance mean?" hint="It is about the source's origins.">
    Who made the source, when and where — its origin.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why is purpose as important as date when judging a source?" hint="A poster and a diary do different jobs.">
    Purpose shapes content: a recruitment poster persuades while a diary records, so the same event looks different in each.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
