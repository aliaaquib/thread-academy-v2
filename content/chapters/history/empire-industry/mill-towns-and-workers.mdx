## Key ideas

### Reform followed suffering

Reports of children working long hours in mills shocked the public. Parliament passed Factory Acts, starting in 1833, limiting children's hours and later setting safety rules. Change came because evidence of suffering forced politicians to act.

### Machines reorganised human life

Before factories, most goods were made at home or in small workshops, and workers set their own hours. The factory brought hundreds of workers under one roof, timed by the clock and the steam engine. Work became faster and cheaper to organise — but also more disciplined and dangerous.

**Key term — Child labour:** Work done by children, common in early factories and mines until laws restricted it.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Mill Towns and Workers">
  Give one reason factory work was hard for children.

  Children worked very long hours, often twelve or more a day, in dangerous conditions.

  **Answer:** Children worked very long hours, often twelve or more a day, in dangerous conditions.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Assuming all workers welcomed machines** Many workers feared machines would destroy their jobs — Luddites smashed machines in protest in the 1810s. New technology created winners and losers.
  - **Saying steam power started the Industrial Revolution** Water power drove the first factories; steam became dominant later. The revolution had several stages, not one starting moment.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="What did the Factory Act of 1833 do?" hint="It limited children's work.">
    It limited the working hours of children in textile mills and introduced inspectors.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What powered the earliest cotton mills?" hint="Think of rivers.">
    Water wheels, using fast-flowing rivers.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why did Britain's mills depend on the wider world?" hint="Cotton does not grow in Lancashire.">
    They needed imported raw cotton and overseas markets to sell finished cloth.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Who were the Luddites?" hint="They protested against machines.">
    Workers in the 1810s who smashed machines they believed were destroying their jobs.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
