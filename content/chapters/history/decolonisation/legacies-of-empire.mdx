## Key ideas

### Freedom brought new challenges

New nations inherited borders drawn by empires, dividing peoples and sowing conflict — partition in 1947 caused massive displacement and violence. Yet independence also brought pride, democracy movements and new voices in world affairs. Historians debate whether decolonisation was a triumph, a tragedy, or — most often — both.

### The Second World War broke the empires

The war bankrupted Britain, shattered the myth of European invincibility — Japan's 1942 capture of Singapore stunned the British Empire — and armed colonial soldiers who returned home demanding rights. Empires that survived the war could not afford, politically or financially, to keep their colonies by force.

**Key term — Decolonisation:** The process by which colonies gained independence from imperial rule, peaking between 1945 and the 1970s.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Legacies of Empire">
  Name two factors that weakened European empires after 1945.

  The financial ruin of the Second World War and the spread of anti-colonial nationalism (also: superpower pressure, the Atlantic Charter's promise of self-determination).

  **Answer:** The financial ruin of the Second World War and the spread of anti-colonial nationalism (also: superpower pressure, the Atlantic Charter's promise of self-determination).
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Assuming independence solved everything** New states faced artificial borders, economic dependence and political instability. Decolonisation began new histories; it did not end them.
  - **Saying Britain simply 'gave' independence** Independence was won through sustained pressure and struggle. 'Granting' independence misdescribes who drove the process.
</ImportantNote>
