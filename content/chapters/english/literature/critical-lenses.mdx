## Key ideas

### Context changes everything

Frankenstein was begun by the eighteen-year-old Mary Shelley during the 1816 Year Without a Summer, amid fierce debates about science and creation. Reading it as a Gothic horror story is valid; reading it as an anxiety about scientific ambition is richer. A-level answers weave context into analysis rather than bolting it on.

### Shakespeare wrote for the stage

His plays are scripts, not novels — meaning lives in performance. A soliloquy is a character thinking aloud to the audience; blank verse gives noble characters a musical rhythm, while prose often marks lower status or madness. Always imagine the scene in the Globe Theatre, not just words on a page.

**Key term — soliloquy:** A speech in which a character speaks their thoughts aloud, alone on stage. Hamlet's 'To be, or not to be' is the most famous example.

<WorkedExample title="Analyse Hamlet's opening line">
  Analyse the opening line of Hamlet's most famous soliloquy: To be, or not to be: that is the question.

  Identify the form: the line opens a soliloquy, so Hamlet speaks his private thoughts directly to the audience.
  Unpack the antithesis: to be (to live) is set against not to be (to die) — the whole speech balances two opposites.
  Notice the rhythm: the measured, near-iambic line gives the thought philosophical weight, as if thinking itself has a heartbeat.
  Connect to the play's concerns: Hamlet debates action versus inaction, the central dilemma of the tragedy.
  Conclude: eleven words state the play's driving question.

  **Answer:** The line's antithesis frames existence as a choice between two opposites, its measured rhythm lends the thought philosophical gravity, and as a soliloquy it draws the audience into Hamlet's private dilemma between action and inaction.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Confusing the narrator with the author** The I of a novel is a constructed voice, not the writer. Analyse what the narrator does, not what the author supposedly felt.
  - **Bolting on context** Context must illuminate the analysis: link the historical detail to a specific quotation or effect, not a separate paragraph of history.
</ImportantNote>
