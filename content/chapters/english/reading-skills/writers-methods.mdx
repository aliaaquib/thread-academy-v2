## Key ideas

### Structure shapes meaning

Writers order their ideas on purpose. A text might open with a shocking image to grab attention, then zoom out to explain it. Noticing where a text begins, shifts and ends lets you comment on structure — a skill GCSE examiners specifically reward.

### Build PEE paragraphs

A strong exam paragraph has three parts: Point — your idea; Evidence — a short quotation; Explanation — how the quotation proves your point. For example, Point: the writer presents the city as threatening. Evidence: 'the streets coiled like snakes'. Explanation: the simile makes the streets seem alive and dangerous, putting the reader on edge.

**Key term — quotation:** The writer's exact words, copied precisely and placed in quotation marks. Short, well-chosen quotations are the evidence that supports your ideas.

<WorkedExample title="Write a PEE paragraph">
  Read this extract, then write a PEE paragraph about how the writer presents the storm. Extract: The sky turned the colour of bruised fruit. Wind tore at the roof tiles, and somewhere a window screamed.

  Point: decide your idea — the writer presents the storm as violent and frightening.
  Evidence: choose a short quotation — 'bruised fruit' or 'a window screamed'.
  Explanation: explain the effect — 'bruised fruit' compares the sky to an injury, suggesting the storm will cause pain; personifying the window as screaming makes the house seem alive and terrified.
  Write the three sentences as one paragraph.
  Check: does every sentence link back to the point?

  **Answer:** The writer presents the storm as violent and frightening. The phrase 'the colour of bruised fruit' compares the sky to an injury, hinting the storm will cause pain. Personifying the window as something that 'screamed' makes the house itself seem terrified, which frightens the reader too.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Feature-spotting without explaining** Naming a simile earns little; explaining its effect on the reader earns the marks. Always ask: what does this make me feel or think?
  - **Forgetting to answer the question** Reread the question after writing. If it asks about the writer's methods, every point should name a method and its effect.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Why might a writer open a text with a short, shocking sentence?" hint="Think about the reader.">
    To grab the reader's attention and create tension from the start.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is an inference?" hint="Think detective.">
    A conclusion drawn from clues in the text, not stated directly.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What do the three letters of PEE stand for?" hint="Point...">
    Point, Evidence, Explanation.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Which is better evidence: the whole sentence 'the dark, gloomy, frightening night' or the single word 'gloomy'?" hint="Shorter is usually stronger.">
    The single word 'gloomy' — short quotations let you focus your analysis.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
