## Key ideas

### Big questions vs everyday questions

'What shall we have for dinner?' is an everyday question, but 'Why is the sky beautiful?' or 'What makes a person good?' are big questions. Big questions have no simple answer and can be thought about for a whole lifetime.

### Questions with no single answer

Some questions, such as 'What happens after we die?', have no answer everyone agrees on. Philosophers do not give up on these questions: they compare different answers and look for the best reasons.

**Key term — Philosophy:** A way of thinking that asks big questions about what exists, what is right, and how we know anything. The word comes from Greek and means 'love of wisdom'.

<WorkedExample title="Puzzling over a big question">
  You ask: 'Is a hot dog a sandwich?' It sounds silly, but answering it properly needs careful thinking.

  Say exactly what the question is asking: what counts as a sandwich?
  List your reasons: a sandwich has food between two pieces of bread. A hot dog has food inside one folded piece of bread.
  Check other cases: what about a burger in a bun? A wrap?
  Decide your answer, but admit what is still unclear.

  **Answer:** There is no perfect answer, and that is fine: you have shown how to think philosophically by defining terms, giving reasons, and testing cases.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Laughing at questions that sound silly** Silly-sounding questions often hide deep ideas. Asking 'why' is never stupid.
  - **Thinking every question has one right answer** Big questions can have several thoughtful answers. The point is which answer has the best reasons behind it.
</ImportantNote>
