## Key ideas

### Deductive validity vs inductive strength

A deductive argument is valid when its form guarantees the conclusion: if you accept the premises, you must accept the conclusion. An inductive argument cannot guarantee its conclusion, so we judge it by strength: how likely do the premises make it? Confusing the two is a classic error — demanding certainty from science, or treating a guess as a proof.

### Necessary is not sufficient

Students often mix these up. Passing your driving test is necessary for driving legally, but passing alone does not make you a good driver: it is not sufficient for skill. In logic and in essays, saying 'X is necessary for Y' is a weaker claim than 'X is sufficient for Y', and examiners reward those who keep them apart.

**Key term — Deduction:** Reasoning in which the conclusion must be true if the premises are true. Example: 'All humans are mortal; Socrates is human; therefore Socrates is mortal.'.

<WorkedExample title="Is this argument deductive or inductive — and is it good?">
  Argument: 'All metals expand when heated. Copper is a metal. Therefore copper expands when heated.' Analyse it step by step.

  Identify the type: the conclusion claims to follow with certainty, so this is deductive.
  Check validity: if the premises are true, the conclusion must be true. The form is valid.
  Check the premises: are they true? Yes — metals do expand when heated, and copper is a metal.
  Conclude: the argument is valid and its premises are true, so it is sound — a good deductive argument.
  Contrast: 'The sun has risen every day, so it will rise tomorrow' is inductive: strong, but not guaranteed.

  **Answer:** The copper argument is a sound deductive argument: valid in form and true in its premises. The sunrise argument is strong but inductive — it gives likelihood, not certainty.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Calling a strong inductive argument 'proof'** Induction gives probability, not proof. Say 'strongly supported', not 'proven', unless the reasoning is deductive.
  - **Swapping necessary and sufficient conditions** 'Oxygen is necessary for fire' does not mean oxygen alone causes fire. Keep 'needs' and 'guarantees' separate.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="What is the difference between deductive and inductive reasoning?" hint="Think certainty versus likelihood.">
    Deduction guarantees the conclusion if the premises are true; induction only makes the conclusion likely.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Is 'All observed ravens are black, so the next raven will be black' deductive or inductive?" hint="Could a white raven exist?">
    Inductive: the premises make it likely, but a white raven remains logically possible.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="In Philippa Foot's trolley problem, what does the lever case test?" hint="Think about what changes between the cases.">
    It tests our moral reasoning: whether we judge by numbers saved, by action versus omission, or by intention.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="A student only reads sources that agree with her essay. Which bias is this, and why is it dangerous?" hint="Its name starts with 'c'.">
    Confirmation bias: seeking only confirming evidence, which makes weak arguments feel strong and hides counter-evidence.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
