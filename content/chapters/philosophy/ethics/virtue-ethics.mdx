## Key ideas

### Aristotle's golden mean

Aristotle argued that each virtue lies between two vices: courage is the mean between cowardice and recklessness, generosity between stinginess and wastefulness. Virtues are built by habit — we become brave by doing brave things — and the virtuous person feels the right emotions at the right time, not just follows rules.

### Hume's is-ought gap

Hume noticed that moral arguments often sneak an 'ought' out of a pile of 'is' statements. Science can tell us that lying breaks trust, but only a moral premise — 'we should not break trust' — yields 'do not lie'. This matters for essays: when you move from facts to moral conclusions, state the moral premise you are relying on.

**Key term — Virtue:** A stable good character trait, such as courage or honesty. For Aristotle, living well means developing virtues through habit.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Virtue Ethics">
  Explain Aristotle's golden mean using courage.

  Courage is the virtue between cowardice (too little) and recklessness (too much); it is the right response for the situation.

  **Answer:** Courage is the virtue between cowardice (too little) and recklessness (too much); it is the right response for the situation.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Calling any moderate behaviour the golden mean** Aristotle's mean is not bland moderation: it is the right amount for the situation. Courage in battle differs from courage in an exam, and both can be demanding.
  - **Deriving 'ought' directly from 'is'** 'Most people do it' never proves 'we should do it'. Always supply the missing moral premise — or admit you do not have one.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="What is the is-ought gap?" hint="Think about Hume and the words 'is' and 'should'.">
    Hume's claim that factual statements about what is cannot by themselves imply moral claims about what ought to be.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="State Kant's categorical imperative in your own words." hint="Think: what if everyone did it?">
    Act only on rules you could rationally want everyone to follow; a rule that contradicts itself when universalised is forbidden.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="How does act utilitarianism differ from rule utilitarianism?" hint="One judges acts, the other judges rules.">
    Act utilitarianism judges each individual act by its own consequences; rule utilitarianism judges whether following a general rule maximises happiness.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Imagine a culture where elders are never questioned. How would a moral relativist and a moral objectivist respond?" hint="Relativism ties morality to culture.">
    The relativist says it is not wrong for that culture, since rightness depends on cultural norms; the objectivist says it can still be wrong, because some moral truths hold universally.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
