Rocks are chemistry written large: every crystal records the conditions of its birth. In this chapter you will decode the silicate structures that build the crust, follow the order of crystallisation in cooling magmas, write the reactions that rot rock away, and use radioactive decay to date the Earth itself.

This lesson focuses on Silicate Minerals: study the silicon-oxygen tetrahedron behind almost every rock-forming mineral.

## Key ideas

### Tetrahedron linkages build mineral families

The silicon-oxygen tetrahedron is the building block of the crust. Olivine keeps its tetrahedra isolated, pyroxene links them into single chains, mica joins them into sheets that peel apart, and quartz shares every oxygen in a rigid three-dimensional framework. The more oxygens are shared, the stronger the mineral and the later it crystallises from magma.

### Cooling magma sorts minerals by temperature

Bowen's reaction series has two branches: the discontinuous branch runs from olivine through pyroxene and amphibole to biotite, while the continuous branch runs from calcium-rich to sodium-rich plagioclase feldspar. Both branches converge on potassium feldspar, muscovite and finally quartz. Early-formed minerals react with the remaining melt as cooling continues, which is why fractional crystallisation can produce very different rocks from one magma.

**Key term — Tetrahedron:** In geochemistry, the silicon-oxygen tetrahedron is one silicon ion bonded to four oxygen ions. How these tetrahedra link together decides whether a mineral forms isolated crystals, chains, sheets or frameworks.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Silicate Minerals">
  Explain why fractional crystallisation can make one magma produce both basalt and granite.

  Early-formed minerals such as olivine are rich in iron and magnesium; if they settle out and are removed, the remaining melt becomes richer in silica, eventually crystallising silica-rich rocks like granite.

  **Answer:** Early-formed minerals such as olivine are rich in iron and magnesium; if they settle out and are removed, the remaining melt becomes richer in silica, eventually crystallising silica-rich rocks like granite.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Assuming weathering is only physical** Physical breakdown is only part of the story. Hydrolysis, oxidation and carbonation chemically transform minerals into new substances such as clays and rust.
  - **Thinking half-life means half the atoms vanish** The atoms do not vanish: each parent atom transforms into a daughter atom. The total number of atoms stays the same; only the ratio changes.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Write a word equation for the oxidation of iron in rocks." hint="Iron plus oxygen plus water gives...">
    Iron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron oxide (rust).
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="In Bowen's reaction series, which mineral crystallises first: olivine or quartz?" hint="Think about temperature.">
    Olivine crystallises first at high temperature; quartz crystallises last at low temperature.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why is quartz harder and more resistant to weathering than olivine?" hint="Think about how the tetrahedra are linked.">
    Quartz shares every oxygen in a strong three-dimensional framework, while olivine has isolated tetrahedra with weaker ionic bonds, so olivine breaks down more easily.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="A sample contains one-quarter of its original parent isotope. How many half-lives have passed?" hint="Halve repeatedly.">
    Two half-lives, since (1/2)² = 1/4.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
