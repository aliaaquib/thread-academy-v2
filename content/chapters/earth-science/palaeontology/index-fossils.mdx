This lesson focuses on Index Fossils: use widespread, short-lived species to match rock layers of the same age.

<Definition term="Index Fossils">
  Use widespread, short-lived species to match rock layers of the same age.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Index fossils correlate distant rocks

If the same index fossil, such as a particular trilobite species, appears in rock layers on different continents, those layers must be the same age, because the species lived everywhere only briefly. This principle of faunal succession lets geologists match up strata worldwide and build the geological time scale without any radiometric dates.

### Fossilisation is a rare accident

Fossilisation requires rapid burial in sediment, which protects remains from scavengers and decay, followed by mineral-rich water seeping through and replacing the original material. Because these conditions are uncommon, the fossil record is biased towards hard parts like bones and shells and towards creatures that lived in seas and lakes where burial was likely.

**Key term — Index fossil:** An index fossil is the fossil of a species that was widespread but existed for a short geological time, making it ideal for dating the rock layer it is found in.

<WorkedExample title="Matching rocks with index fossils">
  A limestone layer in England and a shale layer in France both contain the same species of ammonite, which is known to have existed for only two million years. A student claims the two layers are the same age. Is the student right, and why?

  Check the fossil's credentials: the ammonite was widespread (found in two countries) and short-lived (two million years).
  A short-lived, widespread species makes a good index fossil, because it can only appear in rocks formed during its brief existence.
  Both layers contain the same index fossil, so both must have been deposited while that species was alive.
  Therefore the layers are the same age, even though they are different rock types in different countries.

  **Answer:** Yes. The ammonite is a good index fossil, so any rock containing it formed during the species' short lifetime, meaning both layers are the same age.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Believing humans lived alongside dinosaurs** Non-avian dinosaurs died out about 66 million years ago, while the earliest hominins appeared only a few million years ago. The two never met.
  - **Thinking every dead animal becomes a fossil** Fossilisation needs rapid burial and mineral replacement, which is rare. The vast majority of organisms decay completely and leave no trace.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="What two qualities make a fossil a good index fossil?" hint="Think widespread and brief.">
    It must have been geographically widespread but existed for only a short geological time.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why are most fossils found in sedimentary rather than igneous rocks?" hint="Think about heat and burial.">
    Sedimentary rocks form by gentle burial and layering that preserves remains; igneous rocks form from molten material that destroys them.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Where does fossil evidence say our species originated, and what is one key piece of evidence?" hint="Think Africa and upright walking.">
    Africa; evidence includes early hominin fossils of upright-walking species found in East Africa.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="A rock layer contains trilobites but no dinosaurs. What does this tell you about its relative age?" hint="Trilobites are much older than dinosaurs.">
    It is older than the age of dinosaurs: trilobites dominated long before dinosaurs evolved, so the layer was deposited earlier.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
