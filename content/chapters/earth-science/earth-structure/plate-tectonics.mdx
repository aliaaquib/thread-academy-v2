## Plate Tectonics

The Atlantic Ocean is getting wider by a few centimetres a year. Continents ride on plates of rigid lithosphere, drifting on the slow churn of the mantle.

<Definition term="Plate boundary">
  A **plate boundary** is where tectonic plates meet. At constructive (divergent) boundaries plates move apart; at destructive (convergent) boundaries they collide; at conservative (transform) boundaries they slide past each other.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Constructive boundaries: new crust forms, e.g. the Mid-Atlantic Ridge.
- Destructive boundaries: one plate sinks (subduction) — deep earthquakes, volcanoes, trenches.
- Conservative boundaries: plates grind past — shallow, violent earthquakes like San Andreas.
- Evidence: matching fossils and rock layers across oceans, seafloor spreading, GPS measurements.

<Example title="Japan’s triple junction">
  Japan sits where three plates meet. The 2011 Tōhoku earthquake — magnitude 9.1 — happened at a destructive boundary as one plate lurched beneath another, triggering a devastating tsunami.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Name the three plate boundary types and the motion at each." hint="Apart, together, past.">
    Constructive/divergent: apart. Destructive/convergent: together. Conservative/transform: slide past.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why do volcanoes cluster at destructive boundaries?" hint="What happens to the sinking plate?">
    The subducting plate melts as it sinks, and the magma rises to form volcanoes.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Tectonic plates move a few cm per year on the convecting mantle.
  - Boundary type determines the hazards: ridges, trenches/volcanoes, or fault earthquakes.
  - Fossils, seafloor stripes and GPS all confirm the theory.
  - Most earthquakes and volcanoes sit on plate boundaries.
</Summary>
