The oceans are the engine room of Earth's climate, storing heat and moving it across the planet. In this chapter you will map the great current systems, trace the deep circulation that takes on the order of a thousand years to turn over, explain the rhythm of the tides, and meet El Niño, the climate pattern that rearranges weather worldwide.

This lesson focuses on Ocean Currents: learn how winds, the Coriolis effect and density set surface waters moving.

## Key ideas

### Wind and spin organise surface currents

Prevailing winds drag surface water into huge rotating gyres, and the Coriolis effect bends them into circular paths: clockwise in the northern hemisphere, anticlockwise in the southern. Western boundary currents such as the Gulf Stream are narrow, fast and warm, carrying tropical heat towards the poles and softening the climates of coastlines downstream.

### The deep ocean runs on density

In the North Atlantic, cold salty water becomes dense enough to sink kilometres down, beginning a journey that creeps through the Atlantic, around Antarctica and into the Pacific before rising again. This thermohaline conveyor moves enormous amounts of heat and takes on the order of a thousand years to complete a full loop, which is why it sets the long-term pace of climate change.

**Key term — Coriolis effect:** The Coriolis effect is the apparent deflection of moving water and air caused by Earth's rotation: currents bend to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern hemisphere.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Ocean Currents">
  In which direction do ocean gyres rotate in the northern hemisphere, and why?

  Clockwise, because the Coriolis effect deflects moving water to the right in the northern hemisphere.

  **Answer:** Clockwise, because the Coriolis effect deflects moving water to the right in the northern hemisphere.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Saying the Gulf Stream is just wind** Winds start surface currents, but the Gulf Stream's path and strength also depend on the Coriolis effect, continental shapes and density differences. It is a system, not a single cause.
  - **Thinking tides are caused by the Moon's phases** Tides are caused by gravity, not by how the Moon looks. The phase only matters because a full or new Moon means the Sun and Moon are lined up, strengthening the pull.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="What drives thermohaline circulation?" hint="Think about density.">
    Differences in water density caused by temperature and salinity: cold, salty water sinks and drives the slow deep circulation.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Explain why upwelling zones support rich fisheries." hint="Think about nutrients.">
    Upwelling brings cold, nutrient-rich deep water to the sunlit surface, feeding plankton growth that supports the whole food chain.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why is northwestern Europe milder than other places at the same latitude?" hint="Think about a warm current.">
    The North Atlantic Drift, an extension of the Gulf Stream, carries warm tropical water northward, releasing heat that softens the climate.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why do most coasts get two high tides a day?" hint="Think about the two bulges.">
    There are two tidal bulges, one facing the Moon and one on the opposite side, and Earth's rotation carries each coast through both bulges daily.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
