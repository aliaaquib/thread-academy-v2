## Minerals

Diamond and pencil lead are both pure carbon — the difference is how the atoms are arranged. That arrangement is what makes a mineral.

<Definition term="Mineral">
  A **mineral** is a naturally occurring, inorganic solid with a definite chemical composition and an ordered crystal structure. Rocks are aggregates of one or more minerals.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Identification: hardness (Mohs scale), streak, lustre, cleavage, density.
- Talc (1) can be scratched by a fingernail; diamond (10) scratches everything.
- Ores are rocks rich enough in a mineral to mine profitably.
- Modern life runs on minerals: silicon in chips, lithium in batteries, iron in steel.

<Example title="Fool’s gold">
  Pyrite gleams like gold but is harder, lighter and leaves a greenish-black streak while gold leaves a golden one. Miners learned: test the streak, not the shine.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="A mineral scratches glass but not quartz. What is its approximate Mohs hardness?" hint="Glass ≈ 5.5, quartz = 7.">
    Between 5.5 and 7 — around 6.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why isn’t coal a mineral?" hint="Check the definition: inorganic?">
    Coal forms from decayed plant matter — it’s organic, so it fails the ‘inorganic’ criterion.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Minerals: natural, inorganic, definite chemistry, crystal structure.
  - Hardness, streak, lustre and cleavage identify them.
  - Diamond and graphite show structure matters as much as chemistry.
  - Ores make minerals economically vital.
</Summary>
