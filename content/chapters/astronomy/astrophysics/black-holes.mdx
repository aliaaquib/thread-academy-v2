## Key ideas

### Black holes are simpler than they seem

A black hole is fully described by just mass, spin and charge. Stellar-mass black holes form when massive stars collapse; supermassive ones, millions to billions of times the Sun's mass, sit at the centres of galaxies, including our own Sagittarius A*. In 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope imaged a black hole's shadow in galaxy M87, confirming predictions.

### Stars are balanced between pressure and gravity

A star survives because the outward pressure from fusion in its core exactly balances the inward crush of its own gravity: hydrostatic equilibrium. When fuel runs out, gravity wins and the core contracts, which is why dying stars swell, shed layers or collapse. The H–R diagram organises all of this: a star's position reveals its mass, age and fate.

**Key term — Event horizon:** The boundary around a black hole beyond which nothing, not even light, can escape. It marks the point of no return set by the black hole's mass.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Black Holes">
  What is the event horizon of a black hole?

  The boundary around a black hole inside which the escape velocity exceeds the speed of light, so nothing can escape.

  **Answer:** The boundary around a black hole inside which the escape velocity exceeds the speed of light, so nothing can escape.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Saying black holes suck everything in like vacuum cleaners** Objects orbit black holes normally at safe distances; only crossing the event horizon is fatal. If the Sun became a black hole, Earth's orbit would not change.
  - **Reading the H–R diagram axes the wrong way** Temperature on the H–R diagram increases to the left, the opposite of most graphs; hot blue stars sit on the left, cool red stars on the right.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="What two opposing forces keep a stable star in balance?" hint="One pushes out from fusion, the other pulls in.">
    Outward radiation pressure from nuclear fusion in the core balances the inward pull of the star's own gravity (hydrostatic equilibrium).
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Explain how gravitational lensing helps astronomers find dark matter." hint="Lensing reveals mass, visible or not.">
    Massive objects bend light from background galaxies; by mapping this distortion, astronomers can work out where mass lies, including invisible dark matter.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Where do most stars sit on the H–R diagram, and what does that region represent?" hint="It is a diagonal band.">
    On the main sequence, the diagonal band where stars spend most of their lives fusing hydrogen in their cores.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why does temperature increase to the left on the H–R diagram?" hint="It is just a convention astronomers kept.">
    It is the historical convention: hot, blue stars are plotted on the left and cool, red stars on the right.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
