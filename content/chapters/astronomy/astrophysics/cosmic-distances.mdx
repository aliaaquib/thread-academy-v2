## Key ideas

### The distance ladder

Parallax gives direct distances to nearby stars. Certain stars, like Cepheid variables, pulsate with a period linked to their true brightness, making them standard candles for farther distances. Type Ia supernovae extend the ladder to billions of light years. Each rung is calibrated by the one below, so errors propagate: the whole cosmic distance scale rests on parallax.

### Stars are balanced between pressure and gravity

A star survives because the outward pressure from fusion in its core exactly balances the inward crush of its own gravity: hydrostatic equilibrium. When fuel runs out, gravity wins and the core contracts, which is why dying stars swell, shed layers or collapse. The H–R diagram organises all of this: a star's position reveals its mass, age and fate.

**Key term — Parallax:** The apparent shift of a nearby star against distant background stars as Earth orbits the Sun. Measuring this tiny angle gives the most direct stellar distances, out to a few thousand light years.

<WorkedExample title="Distance from parallax">
  A star has a measured parallax angle of 0.1 arcseconds. Use the relation distance in parsecs = 1 ÷ parallax in arcseconds to find its distance in parsecs and in light years (1 parsec ≈ 3.26 light years).

  Apply the formula: distance = 1 ÷ 0.1 = 10 parsecs.
  Convert to light years: 10 × 3.26 = 32.6 light years.
  Sanity check: a tenth of an arcsecond is a large parallax by astronomical standards, so a nearby distance of tens of light years is sensible.
  Note the inverse relation: halving the parallax angle would double the distance.

  **Answer:** 10 parsecs, about 32.6 light years away.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Treating a parsec as a unit of time** A parsec is a unit of distance (about 3.26 light years), defined from parallax geometry, not a measure of time.
  - **Saying black holes suck everything in like vacuum cleaners** Objects orbit black holes normally at safe distances; only crossing the event horizon is fatal. If the Sun became a black hole, Earth's orbit would not change.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="A Cepheid variable pulsates with a 10-day period, and its period tells us its true luminosity. Outline how this gives its distance." hint="Compare true brightness with apparent brightness.">
    The period gives the star's intrinsic luminosity; comparing this with how bright it appears from Earth yields the distance via the inverse-square law of light.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What two opposing forces keep a stable star in balance?" hint="One pushes out from fusion, the other pulls in.">
    Outward radiation pressure from nuclear fusion in the core balances the inward pull of the star's own gravity (hydrostatic equilibrium).
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Where do most stars sit on the H–R diagram, and what does that region represent?" hint="It is a diagonal band.">
    On the main sequence, the diagonal band where stars spend most of their lives fusing hydrogen in their cores.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why does temperature increase to the left on the H–R diagram?" hint="It is just a convention astronomers kept.">
    It is the historical convention: hot, blue stars are plotted on the left and cool, red stars on the right.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
