## Telescopes

Galileo’s telescope had a lens 3.7 cm wide. Today’s giants have mirrors 39 metres across — and space telescopes escape the atmosphere entirely.

<Definition term="Electromagnetic spectrum">
  Light is just one slice of the **electromagnetic spectrum**, which runs from radio waves through infrared, visible light and ultraviolet to X-rays and gamma rays. Different telescopes catch different slices.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Refractors use lenses; reflectors use mirrors — big research scopes are reflectors.
- Earth’s atmosphere blurs and blocks light, so space telescopes see sharper and further.
- Radio telescopes (like FAST’s 500 m dish) map the invisible universe.
- Each wavelength reveals something different: X-rays show black holes; infrared sees through dust.

<Example title="Hubble vs Webb">
  Hubble (1990) sees visible/ultraviolet light; Webb (2021) sees infrared — peering through dust clouds to the first galaxies. Same idea, different slice of the spectrum, deeper view.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Why put telescopes in space?" hint="What does air do to light?">
    The atmosphere blurs images and blocks many wavelengths; space telescopes avoid both problems.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why are research telescopes reflectors, not refractors?" hint="Think about supporting a huge lens.">
    Huge lenses sag under their own weight and absorb light; mirrors can be supported from behind and made far larger.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Telescopes collect light; bigger apertures see fainter objects.
  - The spectrum runs radio → infrared → visible → ultraviolet → X-ray → gamma.
  - Space telescopes beat atmospheric blurring and blocking.
  - Different wavelengths reveal different cosmic phenomena.
</Summary>
