## The Sun and the Moon

The Sun holds 99.86% of the solar system’s mass. The Moon is a quarter of Earth’s width. Together they choreograph the rhythms of our lives.

<Definition term="Eclipse">
  An **eclipse** happens when one body blocks light to another: a solar eclipse when the Moon blocks the Sun (Moon between Sun and Earth), a lunar eclipse when Earth’s shadow falls on the Moon.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Day and night: Earth spins once every ~24 hours.
- Seasons: Earth’s 23.5° tilt aims each hemisphere toward/away from the Sun through the year — not distance.
- Moon phases: we see the sunlit fraction change over 29.5 days.
- Tides: the Moon’s gravity pulls the oceans into bulges; the Sun modulates them.

<Example title="Why Australia’s summer is our winter">
  In December the southern hemisphere tilts toward the Sun — longer days, more direct light, summer in Sydney while London shivers. Tilt, not distance: Earth is actually closest to the Sun in January.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="What causes the seasons?" hint="It’s the tilt, not the distance.">
    Earth’s 23.5° axial tilt means each hemisphere gets more direct sunlight for half the year.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why don’t we get a solar eclipse every month?" hint="The Moon’s orbit is tilted.">
    The Moon’s orbit is tilted about 5° to Earth’s orbit, so its shadow usually misses Earth.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Rotation gives day/night; tilt gives seasons.
  - The Moon’s phases are a 29.5-day cycle of sunlit fractions.
  - Eclipses need precise Sun–Earth–Moon alignment.
  - Tides come mainly from the Moon’s gravity.
</Summary>
