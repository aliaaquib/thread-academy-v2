## The Life Cycle of Stars

Every atom of carbon in your body, every atom of iron in your blood, was forged in a star. We are, literally, stardust.

<Definition term="Nuclear fusion">
  **Nuclear fusion** is the joining of light atomic nuclei (usually hydrogen into helium) in a star’s core, releasing enormous energy. It needs millions of degrees — which is why it happens in stars, not on tabletops.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Stars form when gravity collapses a nebula until the core ignites.
- Small stars (like the Sun) end as white dwarfs; massive stars explode as supernovae.
- Supernovae forge elements heavier than iron and scatter them — the stuff of planets and people.
- The most massive remnants become neutron stars or black holes.

<Example title="The Sun’s future">
  In about 5 billion years the Sun will swell into a red giant, engulfing Mercury and Venus, then shed its outer layers and fade as a white dwarf — a cooling ember the size of Earth.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Where do elements heavier than iron come from?" hint="Think stellar explosions.">
    They’re forged in supernovae (and neutron-star mergers) and scattered into space.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What determines how a star dies?" hint="It’s about heft.">
    Its mass: Sun-like stars become white dwarfs; massive stars go supernova and leave neutron stars or black holes.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Stars ignite when gravity compresses a nebula’s core enough for fusion.
  - Fusion turns hydrogen into helium, releasing starlight.
  - Mass decides a star’s fate: white dwarf, neutron star or black hole.
  - We are made of stardust — heavy elements from dead stars.
</Summary>
