## Key ideas

### Cultures mix — and sometimes resist

Films, music, brands and food now travel the world, so teenagers in different countries often share tastes. Some people welcome this sharing; others worry it weakens local traditions and languages. In response, many communities actively protect and celebrate their own culture, a trend sometimes called glocalisation — global ideas adapted to local tastes.

### People disagree about globalisation

Supporters argue that globalisation brings cheaper goods, more jobs and faster spread of ideas. Critics argue that it benefits rich countries and big companies more than poor workers, and that it can damage the environment. Scholars are divided too: hyperglobalists say national borders matter less and less, while sceptics say countries and governments still hold the real power.

**Key term — Globalisation:** The process by which businesses, ideas, cultures and people become increasingly connected across the world, mainly through trade, technology and communication.

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Confusing globalisation with westernisation** Although many global brands come from Western countries, cultural flows go in many directions — for example, music, food and films from Asia, Africa and Latin America are popular worldwide.
  - **Saying globalisation is just about trade** Trade is a big part, but globalisation also covers culture, technology, migration and politics. Limiting it to trade misses most of the story.
</ImportantNote>

  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is interdependence? Give an example." hint="It means countries rely on each other.">
    Interdependence is when countries rely on each other — for example, one country depending on another for energy or food — so a problem in one place can affect many others.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is a supply chain?" hint="Think about the journey of a product from raw material to shop.">
