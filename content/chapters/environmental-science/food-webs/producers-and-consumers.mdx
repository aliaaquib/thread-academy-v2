Every animal is connected to others through food: a rabbit eats grass, a fox eats the rabbit, and tiny decomposers recycle them all when they die. Real communities are not neat chains but tangled webs. In this chapter you will learn to read and build food webs, and to predict what happens when they are disturbed.

This lesson focuses on Producers and Consumers: meet the makers and takers in every feeding relationship.

</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Energy flows one way through a web

Food webs show the transfer of energy from producers to consumers. Arrows point from the food to the feeder, showing the direction energy moves. Because energy is lost as heat at each step, webs depend on a constant input from the Sun.

### Webs are more stable than chains, but not unbreakable

In a web, most consumers eat more than one thing, so losing one food source need not be fatal. But removing a key species can still ripple through the web: fewer predators can let prey explode in numbers and overgraze the producers.

**Key term — Trophic level:** A feeding step in a food web. Producers are level one, primary consumers level two, secondary consumers level three, and so on.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Producers and Consumers">
  What is a producer, and why is it at the base of food webs?

  A producer makes its own food using photosynthesis. It sits at the base because it converts light energy into food energy that all consumers ultimately depend on.

  **Answer:** A producer makes its own food using photosynthesis. It sits at the base because it converts light energy into food energy that all consumers ultimately depend on.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Forgetting decomposers** Every food web should include decomposers. Without them, nutrients would never return to the producers.
  - **Drawing food web arrows backwards** Arrows show energy flow, so they point from the food to the feeder: grass → rabbit, not rabbit → grass.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Explain the role of decomposers in a food web." hint="Think about what happens to nutrients in a dead leaf.">
    Decomposers such as fungi and bacteria break down dead organisms and waste, returning nutrients to the soil where producers can absorb and reuse them.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Give one reason a food web is a better model of nature than a single food chain." hint="Think about what real animals actually eat.">
    Real animals usually eat several different foods, and each organism is eaten by several predators. A web captures these multiple feeding links, while a chain shows only one path.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Draw or describe a four-step food chain for a woodland and label each trophic level." hint="Start with a plant and end with a top predator.">
    Example: oak leaves (producer, level 1) → caterpillar (primary consumer, level 2) → blue tit (secondary consumer, level 3) → sparrowhawk (tertiary consumer, level 4).
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why do arrows in a food web point from the prey to the predator?" hint="The arrows track something flowing.">
    Arrows show the direction of energy flow, which moves from the food organism into the feeder.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
