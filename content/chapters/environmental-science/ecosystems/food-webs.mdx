## Food Chains and Food Webs

“You are what you eat” is almost literally true in ecology. Every organism is a link in a chain of energy transfer, and those chains tangle into webs.

<Definition term="Trophic level">
  A **trophic level** is an organism’s position in a food chain: producers (level 1), primary consumers (level 2), secondary consumers (level 3), and so on. Decomposers recycle nutrients at every level.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Producers convert sunlight to chemical energy; everything else is a consumer.
- Only about 10% of energy transfers between trophic levels — the rest is lost as heat and movement.
- This is why food chains rarely exceed 4–5 levels.
- Removing a keystone species can collapse the whole web.

<Example title="Wolves return to Yellowstone">
  When wolves were reintroduced, elk numbers fell and their grazing patterns changed. Willows recovered, beavers returned, riverbanks stabilised. One predator reshaped rivers — a trophic cascade through the food web.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="In grass → rabbit → fox, which is the producer and which trophic level is the fox?" hint="Count up from the producer.">
    Grass is the producer (level 1); the rabbit is the primary consumer (level 2); the fox is the secondary consumer (level 3).
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why are there usually fewer top predators than producers?" hint="Think about the 10% rule.">
    Energy is lost at each transfer, so less energy reaches higher levels — fewer organisms can be supported.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Food webs show the feeding relationships in an ecosystem.
  - Energy transfer between levels is inefficient (~10%).
  - Decomposers return nutrients to producers.
  - Keystone species hold webs together disproportionately.
</Summary>
