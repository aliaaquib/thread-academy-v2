Every country's most important rules are the ones that say how power itself is organised. A constitution sets out who makes the laws, what limits leaders face and which rights citizens hold. This chapter explains what constitutions do, compares written and unwritten models, and shows how they can be changed.

This lesson focuses on What Is a Constitution: learn how a constitution sets the basic rules for how a country is governed.

## Key ideas

### A constitution sets the rules about rules

Ordinary laws say what citizens may or may not do, but a constitution says who gets to make those laws and what limits apply. It usually defines the roles of the legislature, executive and judiciary and protects basic rights. Because it sits above ordinary law, changing it is normally harder than passing a normal statute.

### Written and unwritten constitutions

Some countries, like the United States, have a single written constitutional document adopted in 1787 and ratified in 1788. The United Kingdom has an unwritten constitution made up of statutes, conventions, court judgements and authoritative works. Both approaches can work; the key question is whether the rules are clear, accepted and enforceable.

**Key term — constitution:** The body of basic rules that establishes how a state is governed, allocates power among institutions, and protects fundamental rights. It stands above ordinary law and is normally harder to change.

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Thinking a constitution can never be changed** Most constitutions include an amendment process precisely because societies change. The US Constitution has been amended to abolish slavery and extend voting rights. The difficulty of amendment is a design choice to protect stability, not a ban on change.
  - **Claiming the United Kingdom has no constitution** The UK has no single written constitutional document, but it does have a constitution made of statutes such as the Bill of Rights of 1689, legal conventions, court judgements and parliamentary practice. Unwritten does not mean non-existent.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Describe the US Constitution and explain how it limits government." hint="Give the dates, then the mechanisms of limited government.">
    It was adopted in 1787 and ratified in 1788, creating a federal system with separated powers and checks and balances. It limits government through enumerated powers, a bill of rights in its amendments, and judicial review of laws against the Constitution.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Explain what is meant by the United Kingdom's unwritten constitution." hint="List the sources, then explain parliamentary sovereignty.">
    The UK constitution rests on statutes, conventions, judicial decisions and authoritative commentary rather than one document. Parliamentary sovereignty means Parliament can make or unmake any law, which makes the constitution flexible but relies on political and legal culture to restrain power.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is a constitutional amendment, and why is the US amendment process demanding?" hint="Define it, then give the two supermajority stages.">
    An amendment is a formal change to a constitution's text. The US process needs two-thirds of Congress to propose and three-quarters of the states to ratify, so amendments require broad, lasting agreement rather than a temporary majority.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Explain the rule of law and its link to constitutional government." hint="Start with equality before the law, then link to constitutional limits.">
    The rule of law means everyone, including the government, is subject to the law, which must be clear, public and fairly applied. It connects to constitutions because constitutional limits and independent courts stop rulers from acting above the law.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
