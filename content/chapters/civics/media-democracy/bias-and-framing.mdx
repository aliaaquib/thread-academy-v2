## Key ideas

### Bias and propaganda are different problems

Bias is the slant that comes from selection, word choice and framing; it can be unconscious and exists even in serious journalism. Propaganda is the deliberate use of misleading or emotional messages to manipulate opinion, and it may come from governments, parties or commercial actors. Citizens need different responses: read across sources to manage bias, and verify claims to resist propaganda.

### Ownership and regulation shape the news environment

When a few owners control many outlets, the range of viewpoints can narrow even without direct censorship. Regulation, public broadcasters and competition rules are different democratic attempts to protect pluralism. Evaluating a media system means asking who owns the outlets, who funds them and what rules constrain them.

**Key term — framing:** The way an issue is presented through word choice, images, headlines and emphasis so as to shape how audiences interpret it. The same facts can support very different impressions depending on the frame.

</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Equating impartiality with having no viewpoint** Every newsroom makes judgements about what to cover and how to frame it. Impartiality means fair treatment of evidence and perspectives, not the absence of editorial judgement. Demanding viewpoint-free news is impossible; demanding honest, checkable reporting is not.
  - **Thinking press freedom means freedom from all rules** Even free media systems have laws on libel, privacy, contempt of court and broadcasting standards. Press freedom means journalists can investigate power without intimidation or censorship, not that anything may be published without consequence.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Distinguish media bias from propaganda." hint="Contrast slant with deliberate manipulation.">
    Bias is slant in selection, emphasis or language, which can be unintentional. Propaganda is deliberate manipulation through misleading or emotive messaging to serve an agenda. Bias calls for reading across sources; propaganda calls for verifying claims and identifying techniques.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Analyse how concentrated media ownership can threaten democratic debate." hint="Think about viewpoint diversity and who sets the agenda.">
    Concentrated ownership can narrow the range of viewpoints, align coverage with owners' interests and reduce investigative challenge to powerful allies. Pluralism can be protected through competition rules, public service broadcasters and transparent ownership.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Define press freedom and explain why democracy needs it." hint="Link the journalist's freedom to the citizen's need.">
    The right of journalists to report, investigate and criticise without censorship or intimidation. It matters because citizens need reliable information to vote, debate and hold power to account; without it, misconduct stays hidden.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="List four propaganda techniques and explain one of them." hint="Name several techniques, then explain one fully.">
    Name-calling, glittering generalities, transfer, testimonial, plain folks, card stacking and bandwagon appeals. Card stacking, for example, presents only favourable facts while hiding the rest, which citizens can counter by seeking the missing evidence.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
