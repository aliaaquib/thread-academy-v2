## Being an Active Citizen

The suffragettes couldn’t vote — so they marched, petitioned and went to prison. Civic power has many channels.

<Definition term="Civil society">
  **Civil society** is the space between the individual and the state — charities, clubs, unions, faith groups, campaigns. It’s where citizens organise to solve problems governments can’t or won’t.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Vote when you can — and register.
- Petitions, peaceful protest, contacting representatives — all protected in democracies.
- Volunteering and community groups: direct action, no permission needed.
- Digital citizenship: share responsibly; misinformation is a civic harm.

<Example title="The litter-pick that changed a council">
  Residents who started picking litter documented fly-tipping, petitioned the council with photos, and got enforcement funded. Small action → evidence → policy.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Name three ways to participate in civic life without voting." hint="Voice, hands, wallet.">
    Petitioning/protesting, volunteering, contacting representatives, joining campaigns or community groups.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why is sharing misinformation a civic problem?" hint="Democracy needs shared facts.">
    Democratic decisions need accurate information; misinformation distorts debate and can suppress or manipulate votes.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Civic power isn’t only voting.
  - Civil society sits between individuals and the state.
  - Small, documented actions can change policy.
  - Responsible information-sharing is a civic duty.
</Summary>
