## How Laws Are Made

A bill is a proposed law. Turning one into statute is deliberately hard — friction that filters bad ideas (and sometimes good ones).

<Definition term="Bill">
  A **bill** is a draft law under consideration by a legislature. It becomes an **act/statute** only after passing the required readings, committee scrutiny and votes — plus executive assent.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Introduction/first reading: the idea is tabled.
- Committee stage: line-by-line scrutiny — where detail is hammered out.
- Debate and votes in each chamber (in bicameral systems).
- Assent: the head of state signs it into law. Citizens can lobby, petition and give evidence at committee stage.

<Example title="A bill’s obstacle course (UK)">
  Commons first reading → second reading debate → committee → report → third reading — then the whole thing again in the Lords, then Royal Assent. Most bills die somewhere along the way.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Why do bills face multiple readings and a committee stage?" hint="Deliberate friction.">
    To allow scrutiny, debate and amendment — catching flaws and building consent before a proposal gains the force of law.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="At which stage can ordinary citizens most easily influence a bill?" hint="Evidence and lobbying.">
    Committee stage (submitting evidence) and through lobbying representatives before key votes — plus public campaigns shaping the debate.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Bills become law through readings, committees and votes.
  - Scrutiny stages exist to catch flaws.
  - Bicameral systems repeat the process in two chambers.
  - Citizens can intervene via evidence, lobbying and campaigns.
</Summary>
