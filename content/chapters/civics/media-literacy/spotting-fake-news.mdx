## Key ideas

### Check before you trust

Reliable readers look at the source, the date, the evidence and whether other outlets agree. A dramatic claim with no named source, no date and no supporting evidence deserves scepticism. Two minutes of checking can stop a false story spreading further.

### News is made, not just found

Journalists choose which events to cover, whom to interview, what to put in the headline and which pictures to use. These choices are shaped by deadlines, evidence and editorial judgement. Knowing that news is constructed helps readers ask what was left out, not just what was included.

**Key term — misinformation:** False or misleading information shared without deliberate intent to deceive, such as an honestly believed rumour. It still causes harm, which is why checking before sharing matters.

<WorkedExample title="The Viral School Closure Post">
  A classmate shares a post claiming your school will close for a whole week, but the post has no date and names no source. What should you do before sharing it?

  Step 1: Pause. Dramatic claims deserve checking, not instant sharing.
  Step 2: Inspect the post. Look for a date, an author and a link to an official source.
  Step 3: Check official channels. Look at the school website or official messages for confirmation.
  Step 4: Compare. See whether trusted local news or other parents report the same thing.
  Step 5: Decide. Share only if an official source confirms it; otherwise warn the classmate it may be false.

  **Answer:** Do not share it yet. The post gives no date, no official source and no evidence, and the school website says nothing. The responsible move is to check the school's official channels or ask a teacher, then share only confirmed information. Sharing first and checking later risks spreading a false rumour.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Thinking bias means lying** Bias is about slant in what is covered and how it is presented, which can happen without inventing facts. A report can be factually correct yet misleading through selective emphasis. Spotting bias means asking what is missing, not just what is false.
  - **Assuming popular posts must be true** Likes and shares measure attention, not accuracy. False stories often spread faster because they are surprising or emotional. Always check the source rather than counting shares.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Describe three habits of a responsible sharer online." hint="Think pause, check, then share.">
    Pause before sharing, check the claim with a reliable source, consider who benefits from you sharing it, and correct the record kindly if you shared something false. Responsible sharing protects both your reputation and public debate.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is a news source, and why does it matter?" hint="Who is telling you this?">
    The person or organisation behind a story, such as a named journalist, official body or eyewitness. Knowing the source lets you judge expertise, motives and reliability.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="List four checks for deciding whether a story is trustworthy." hint="Give four quick checks.">
    Check the author and outlet, look for a date, find the original evidence, and see whether other reliable outlets report it. If these checks fail, treat the claim as unconfirmed.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Distinguish misinformation from disinformation." hint="Focus on whether the sharer meant to deceive.">
    Misinformation is false information shared without intent to deceive, such as passing on a rumour you believed. Disinformation is false information created or shared deliberately to mislead. Intent is the key difference.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
