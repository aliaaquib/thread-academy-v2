## Misinformation

You’re more likely to be misled by a confident friend’s share than by a sinister bot farm. Most misinformation is spread by ordinary people who believe it.

<Definition term="Lateral reading">
  **Lateral reading**: instead of digging deeper into a dubious site, open new tabs and check what reputable sources say about the claim AND the site. Fact-checkers read across the web, not down the page.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Misinformation: false, spread without intent to deceive. Disinformation: deliberately deceptive.
- Why it spreads: novelty, emotion, identity — false stories are engineered to trigger shares.
- Red flags: ALL CAPS outrage, no named sources, ‘they don’t want you to know’.
- Habit: pause, lateral-read, check the date — then decide whether to share.

<Example title="The old photo, new claim">
  A dramatic photo of a ‘protest today’ turns out to be from 2015 in another country. Reverse image search takes ten seconds — and kills most viral fakes.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Distinguish misinformation from disinformation." hint="Intent.">
    Misinformation is false but spread without deceptive intent; disinformation is deliberately created to mislead.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is lateral reading, and when should you use it?" hint="New tabs, not deeper scrolling.">
    Checking a claim across independent reputable sources in new tabs — use it whenever a story triggers strong emotion or comes from an unfamiliar source.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Misinformation is mistaken; disinformation is malicious.
  - Falsehoods exploit emotion and identity.
  - Lateral reading beats deep reading of dubious sites.
  - Pause before sharing — virality rewards the unthinking.
</Summary>
