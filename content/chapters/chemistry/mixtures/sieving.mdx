<Definition term="Separating by Sieving">
  Use a sieve to separate solids of different sizes, like flour from lumps.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Choose the method that fits

Each separation method suits a particular mixture: sieving separates solids of different sizes, filtering separates an insoluble solid from a liquid, and evaporation recovers a dissolved solid from its solution. Scientists look at the properties of the parts — size, solubility, state — and then pick the method. Using the wrong method, like trying to sieve salt water, simply does not work.

### Mixtures can be undone

In a mixture, the materials are jumbled together but not joined — a bowl of sand and water is still sand plus water. Because nothing new has been made, mixtures can usually be separated back into their parts. This is different from baking, where the ingredients change into something new.

**Key term — sieve:** A mesh that lets small pieces through but holds back bigger ones, used to separate solids by size.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Separating by Sieving">
  Which method would separate pebbles from sand: sieving, filtering or evaporating?

  Sieving — the sand falls through the holes but the pebbles are too big.

  **Answer:** Sieving — the sand falls through the holes but the pebbles are too big.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Thinking sieving works for liquids** A sieve separates solids by size — small pieces fall through the holes. It cannot separate two liquids, and it cannot separate a dissolved solid from a liquid.
  - **Trying to filter a dissolved solid** Filter paper traps solid lumps but dissolved salt passes straight through with the water. To get dissolved salt back, evaporate the water instead.
</ImportantNote>
