Every day you make economic choices without realising it: which snack to buy, which game to play, how to spend your pocket money. This chapter shows why we can never have everything we want, and how understanding needs, wants and scarcity helps us make smarter choices.

This lesson focuses on Needs vs Wants: learn the difference between things we must have to live and things we would simply like.

<Definition term="Needs vs Wants">
  Learn the difference between things we must have to live and things we would simply like.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Wise spending and saving

Money you save today can be spent later, while money spent is gone. Wise spenders compare needs and wants before buying, wait before buying expensive wants, and keep some money aside for the future.

### The basic economic problem

People have unlimited wants but limited resources. Even the richest person cannot have everything, because time, money and raw materials are all limited. This mismatch between wants and resources is called scarcity, and it is the starting point of all economics.

**Key term — need:** Something you must have to live a healthy life, such as food, water, shelter and clothing. Without your needs met, you cannot survive or stay well.

<WorkedExample title="Ali's £10 dilemma">
  Ali has £10 of birthday money. He needs a new water bottle for school (£6) and wants a comic book (£5). Use opportunity cost to decide.

  List Ali's needs: the water bottle is a need because he uses it every day at school.
  List Ali's wants: the comic is a want — nice to have, but he can live without it.
  Add the costs: £6 + £5 = £11, which is more than the £10 available — scarcity strikes.
  Apply opportunity cost: buying the bottle means giving up the comic; buying the comic still leaves him unable to afford the bottle.
  Decide: buy the bottle (£6), save the remaining £4 towards the comic later — needs come first.

  **Answer:** Ali cannot afford both because of scarcity. The opportunity cost of buying the water bottle is the comic book he gives up, so the wise choice is to buy the need first and save the rest.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Thinking wants are needs** A games console is a want, not a need — you can live without it, even if everyone else has one.
  - **Forgetting that saving is also a choice** Money saved is not money lost — it stays available for future needs and wants.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Why do economists say wants are unlimited?" hint="Think about what happens when you get something new.">
    Because as soon as one want is satisfied, new ones appear — there is always something else people would like.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Which of these is a need: a warm coat, a video game, or a sticker album?" hint="Think about what you must have to stay healthy.">
    A warm coat — it protects you from the cold; the others are wants.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Sara has £8. A book costs £5 and ice cream costs £4. Can she buy both?" hint="Add the two prices together.">
    No — together they cost £9, which is more than £8. Scarcity means she must choose.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Tom saves £2 of his £5 pocket money each week. Give one reason saving is sensible." hint="Think about the future.">
    Saved money is available later for bigger needs or unexpected costs — it buys future choices.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
