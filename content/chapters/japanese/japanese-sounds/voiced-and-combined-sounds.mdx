## Key ideas

### Marks that change sounds

The dakuten ゛ voices a sound: は (ha) → ば (ba). The handakuten ゜, a small circle, makes the p-sound: は (ha) → ぱ (pa). A small っ (sokuon) doubles the following consonant, so さか (saka, slope) and さっか (sakka, author) are different words. Watch these tiny marks closely — they change meaning.

### One kana equals one syllable

English letters spell sounds piece by piece, but each kana is a complete syllable. The word さくら (sakura, cherry blossom) is just three kana: さ (sa), く (ku) and ら (ra). There are 46 basic hiragana, arranged in the traditional gojūon (fifty-sounds) order that starts あいうえお (a i u e o). Learn the rows in order and new characters slot neatly into place.

**Key term — ようおん (yōon):** Combined sounds made by adding a small ゃ, ゅ or ょ to a kana. For example, き (ki) plus small ゃ becomes きゃ (kya), as in きゃく (kyaku, guest).

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Voiced and Combined Sounds">
  What does the dakuten ゛ do to た (ta)?

  It turns た (ta) into だ (da).

  **Answer:** It turns た (ta) into だ (da).
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Reading が (ga) as か (ka)** The dakuten ゛ is small but mighty. がっこう (gakkō) means school, while かっこう (kakkō) means appearance — always check for the two little strokes.
  - **Skipping the small っ** きて (kite, come and see) and きって (kitte, stamp) differ only in the small っ, which doubles the t-sound. Pause briefly on it instead of gliding past.
</ImportantNote>
