## Key ideas

### Counting is musical

Arabic numbers from one to ten have a lovely rhythm: واحد، اثنان، ثلاثة، أربعة، خمسة، ستة، سبعة، ثمانية، تسعة، عشرة (wāḥid, ithnān, thalātha, arbaʿa, khamsa, sitta, sabʿa, thamāniya, tisʿa, ʿashara). Chant them, clap them, bounce a ball to them — rhythm makes them stick. Eleven and twelve are built from 'one-ten' and 'two-ten': أحد عشر (aḥada ʿashara) and اثنا عشر (ithnā ʿashara).

### Colours change for masculine and feminine nouns

Arabic colours have a masculine and a feminine form. Masculine: أحمر، أزرق، أخضر، أصفر، أسود، أبيض. Feminine nouns take the ـاء ending: حمراء، زرقاء، خضراء، صفراء، سوداء، بيضاء. So it is قميص أحمر (qamīṣ aḥmar, a red shirt) but سيارة حمراء (sayyāra ḥamrāʾ, a red car), because 'shirt' is masculine and 'car' is feminine.

**Key term — واحد (wāḥid) — one:** The number 1 and the starting point of all counting. To say 'one book' you say كتاب واحد (kitāb wāḥid).

<WorkedExample title="Counting three books">
  Write 'three books' in Arabic.

  'Three' is ثلاثة (thalātha).
  'Book' is كتاب (kitāb); 'books' is كتب (kutub).
  In Arabic the number comes before the noun: ثلاثة كتب.
  Say it smoothly: the ة is pronounced as 't' here — thalāthatu kutub.

  **Answer:** ثلاثة كتب (thalātha kutub) — 'three books'.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Putting the colour before the noun** English says 'red car'; Arabic says سيارة حمراء — the noun سيارة comes first, then the colour.
  - **Using أحمر for a feminine noun** سيارة is feminine, so 'red car' needs the feminine حمراء: سيارة حمراء. Masculine أحمر only pairs with masculine nouns like قميص.
</ImportantNote>
