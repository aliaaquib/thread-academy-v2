## Key ideas

### 'My' is just a tail on the word

Arabic does not need a separate word for 'my' — you glue ـي to the end of the noun. كتاب (kitāb, book) becomes كتابي (kitābī, my book); اسم (ism, name) becomes اسمي (ismī, my name). When the noun ends in ة, the ة opens into ت first: مدرسة becomes مدرستي (madrasatī, my school). One tiny tail, endless new words.

### He-words and she-words

Every Arabic noun is masculine or feminine, and adjectives must match. Feminine nouns often (but not always) end in ة: معلمة (muʿallima, female teacher), صديقة (ṣadīqa, female friend). So you say صديقي مجتهد (ṣadīqī mujtahid, my friend [male] is hardworking) but صديقتي مجتهدة (ṣadīqatī mujtahida, my friend [female] is hardworking). Watch the ة — it is your best clue.

**Key term — مدرسة (madrasa) — school:** A feminine noun (note the ة). 'My school' is مدرستي (madrasatī) — the ة turns into a ت before the ـي.

<WorkedExample title="Saying 'I like my school'">
  Build the sentence 'I like my school' in Arabic.

  'I like' is أحب (uḥibbu).
  'School' is مدرسة (madrasa); 'my school' is مدرستي (madrasatī) — the ة becomes ت before ـي.
  Put the verb first, as Arabic usually does: أحب مدرستي.
  Read it aloud: u-ḥib-bu mad-ra-sa-tī.

  **Answer:** أحب مدرستي (uḥibbu madrasatī) — 'I like my school'.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Writing مدرسةي for 'my school'** The ة cannot wear the ـي tail directly — it turns into ت first: مدرستي (madrasatī).
  - **Saying هو for 'she'** هو (huwa) is 'he'; هي (hiya) is 'she'. Mixing them up confuses every sentence about your family.
</ImportantNote>
