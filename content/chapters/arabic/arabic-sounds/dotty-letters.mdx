## Key ideas

### Reading right to left

Arabic is written and read from right to left, the mirror image of English. The first letter of a word sits on the right-hand side, and your eyes travel leftwards across the page. Numbers, though, still run left to right, so ٢٠٢٦ reads just like 2026. Practise by pointing at each letter from right to left as you sound it out.

### Letters change their shape

Most Arabic letters change shape depending on where they sit in a word: at the start, in the middle, at the end, or all alone. The letter ع, for example, is a neat loop on its own, grows a tooth at the start of a word, stretches both ways in the middle and curls off at the end, as in علم (ʿilm, knowledge). It is always the same letter with the same sound — only its outfit changes. Learn the four shapes and every word becomes readable.

**Key term — ا (ʾalif) — alif:** The first letter of the Arabic alphabet. It is a tall straight line and makes a long 'aa' sound, as in باب (bāb, door).

<WorkedExample title="Reading the word باب">
  Sound out the word باب and work out what it means.

  Start on the right. The first letter is ب (bāʾ), making a 'b' sound.
  Next comes ا (alif), making a long 'aa' sound. Bāʾ joins to it, so the two letters connect.
  Alif is one of the six letters that never join to the next letter, so the final ب stands on its own.
  Put the sounds together from right to left: b + aa + b = bāb.

  **Answer:** باب is read 'bāb' and means 'door'.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Joining a letter after ا د ذ ر ز و** These six letters never connect to the letter on their left, the next one as you read. Leave a small gap, as in دار (dār, house), where every letter stands apart.
  - **Mixing up ب ت ث** These three letters share one shape — only the dots differ. ب has one dot below, ت has two dots above and ث has three dots above. Count the dots every time.
</ImportantNote>
