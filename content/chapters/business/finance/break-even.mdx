<Definition term="Break-Even Analysis">
  Calculate the sales needed to avoid losses.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Break-even guides decisions

Break-even output = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit. Below it the firm loses money; above it, profit. The margin of safety (actual sales − break-even) shows how far sales can fall before losses begin — vital for risky launches.

### Profit is revenue minus costs

Total costs = fixed + variable costs. Profit = total revenue − total costs. Raising prices or cutting costs both boost profit — but cutting quality to cut costs can destroy revenue, so finance decisions ripple through the whole business.

**Key term — break-even point:** The output where total revenue equals total costs, calculated as fixed costs ÷ (price − variable cost per unit).

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Break-Even Analysis">
  What is the margin of safety?

  Actual sales minus break-even output — how far sales can fall before losses.

  **Answer:** Actual sales minus break-even output — how far sales can fall before losses.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Confusing profit with cash** Profit includes credit sales not yet paid for — a firm can be profitable and still run out of cash.
  - **Ignoring fixed costs in pricing** Price must cover variable costs and contribute to fixed costs, or each sale deepens the loss.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Calculate break-even: fixed costs £5,000, price £10, variable cost £6." hint="5000 ÷ (10 − 6).">
    1,250 units (£5,000 ÷ £4).
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="A firm's break-even is 400 units and it sells 550. What is the margin of safety?" hint="Subtract.">
    150 units (550 − 400).
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Rent is £1,000 a month whatever is produced. Fixed or variable cost?" hint="Does it change with output?">
    Fixed cost.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why can a profitable business fail?" hint="Think about timing of payments.">
    Poor cash flow — e.g. customers pay late while suppliers demand cash now (overtrading).
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
