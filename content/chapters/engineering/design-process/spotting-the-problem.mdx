<Definition term="Spotting the Problem">
  Define exactly who needs what before designing anything.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Start with the problem, not the solution

Engineers begin by writing a design brief that states who the user is, what they need, and what limits apply. A brief for a school bag might demand it carries 5 kg of books, fits a 30 cm folder, and stays within a set budget. Skipping this step leads to elegant solutions to the wrong problem.

### Many ideas beat one idea

Engineers sketch lots of rough ideas quickly instead of falling in love with the first one. Each sketch can borrow the best features of the others, and weak ideas are cheap to discard on paper. The chosen concept is then developed into detailed drawings with real dimensions.

**Key term — Design brief:** A short statement of the problem, the user, and the requirements the solution must meet.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Spotting the Problem">

  **Answer:** A short statement of the problem, the user, and the requirements the solution must meet.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Starting to build before writing a brief** Without a brief you cannot tell whether the finished design is any good. Define the requirements first, then design to meet them.
  - **Falling in love with the first sketch** The first idea is rarely the best. Sketch several alternatives and compare them honestly before choosing.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="An engineer sketches six ideas for a bridge and picks the best parts of three. What stage is this?" hint="It happens before prototyping.">
    The ideation or concept stage: generating and combining ideas before building anything.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why do engineers build prototypes instead of going straight to the final product?" hint="Think about the cost of a mistake.">
    Prototypes are cheap, rough models that reveal flaws early, when changes cost little.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="A specification says a shelf must hold 10 kg. It breaks at 8 kg in testing. What should happen next?" hint="The test revealed something useful.">
    Redesign and strengthen the shelf, then test again: iterate until it meets the 10 kg requirement.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Give two things a design specification for a water bottle might include." hint="Think about measurable requirements.">
    Any two measurable requirements, for example: holds 750 ml, keeps water cold for 6 hours, weighs less than 300 g, or stays within a set price.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
