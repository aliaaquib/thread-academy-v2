This lesson focuses on Mechanical Advantage: machines multiply force, but they never create energy.

<Definition term="Mechanical Advantage">
  Machines multiply force, but they never create energy.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Machines multiply force, not energy

A machine lets a small effort balance a large load, and the ratio of load to effort is its mechanical advantage. But the effort must move farther than the load moves, so the work in equals the work out, minus losses. No machine can output more energy than is put in: that would break the conservation of energy.

### Compound machines chain the gains

Real machines link simple machines in series: in a bicycle, the pedals form a lever, the chainring and sprocket form gears, and the wheels act as rolling levers. The overall mechanical advantage is the product of each stage's advantage. Analysing a complex machine means breaking it into its simple stages and multiplying.

**Key term — Mechanical advantage:** How many times a machine multiplies the input force: load divided by effort.

<WorkedExample title="Lifting with a lever">
  A 1.5 m crowbar is used to lift a heavy crate. The fulcrum sits 0.3 m from the crate end, and a worker pushes down with an effort of 50 N at the far end, 1.2 m from the fulcrum. What load can be lifted, and what is the mechanical advantage?

  Balance the moments about the fulcrum: effort times effort arm = load times load arm.
  Effort moment = 50 N times 1.2 m = 60 N m.
  Load arm = 0.3 m, so load = 60 N m divided by 0.3 m = 200 N.
  Mechanical advantage = load divided by effort = 200 N divided by 50 N = 4.
  Check the trade: the worker's end moves 4 times farther than the crate rises, so work in equals work out.

  **Answer:** The lever can lift a 200 N load with a mechanical advantage of 4. The worker trades a longer push for a multiplied force, and no energy is created.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Claiming a machine creates energy** Machines only transform energy and trade force for distance. Useful output energy can never exceed input energy.
  - **Mixing up mechanical advantage and efficiency** Mechanical advantage compares forces; efficiency compares energies. A machine can have a huge mechanical advantage yet poor efficiency if friction wastes much of the input.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="A machine lifts an 800 N load with a 200 N effort. What is its mechanical advantage?" hint="Divide load by effort.">
    4, because 800 divided by 200 = 4.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Two gear stages each give a mechanical advantage of 3. What is the overall advantage?" hint="Stages in series multiply.">
    9, because 3 times 3 = 9.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why can no machine be 100 per cent efficient?" hint="What happens where parts rub?">
    Friction always converts some input energy into wasted heat and sound.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="A ball bearing reduces friction in a wheel. How does this change the machine's efficiency?" hint="Less wasted energy means what for the output?">
    Efficiency rises, because less input energy is wasted as heat, leaving more as useful output.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
