## Forces in Structures

A bridge holds a traffic jam without noticing. It does it by channelling every newton of load through its members and into the ground.

<Definition term="Load path">
  The **load path** is the route forces take through a structure to the ground. Good design gives loads a short, strong path; bad design leaves them nowhere safe to go.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Tension pulls members apart (cables, ropes); compression squeezes them (columns, arches).
- Most materials are weaker in tension than compression — hence reinforced concrete.
- Triangulation turns bending forces into pure tension/compression.
- Structures fail when a load exceeds a member’s strength — or when the design ignores a load (wind, earthquakes).

<Example title="The Tacoma Narrows collapse (1940)">
  A suspension bridge twisted itself apart in wind — not from weak materials but from aerodynamic flutter the designers never considered. Loads you ignore still exist.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Is a hanging cable in tension or compression?" hint="Is it being pulled or squeezed?">
    Tension — its weight pulls it longer.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why are bridge trusses full of triangles?" hint="What do triangles do to bending?">
    Triangles are rigid: they convert loads into tension and compression in the members instead of bending.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Tension stretches; compression squeezes.
  - Loads must have a safe path to the ground.
  - Triangles make structures rigid.
  - Design for ALL loads — including wind and quakes.
</Summary>
