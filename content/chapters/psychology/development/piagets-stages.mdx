## Key ideas

### Piaget: children think in stages

Piaget proposed four invariant stages: sensorimotor (0–2), where infants develop object permanence — the understanding that hidden things still exist; preoperational (2–7), marked by egocentrism and a failure to conserve quantity; concrete operational (7–11), where logical thought about real objects appears; and formal operational (11+), bringing abstract and hypothetical reasoning. He saw children as little scientists, actively constructing understanding by assimilating new experiences into schemas and accommodating the schemas when they do not fit. Critics note he underestimated young children and that the stages are less rigid than he claimed.

### Vygotsky: learning is social first

Vygotsky argued that cognitive development is driven by social interaction: children learn in the zone of proximal development, tackling tasks just beyond their solo reach with help from a more knowledgeable other. Scaffolding — hints, modelling and feedback that fade as competence grows — turns today's assisted performance into tomorrow's independent skill. Unlike Piaget's lone scientist, Vygotsky's child learns through culture and conversation, which is why teaching and collaboration matter so much.

**Key term — schema:** A mental framework for organising knowledge, which Piaget said children build and update as they learn.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Piaget's Stages">
  Name Piaget's four stages of cognitive development with their approximate ages.

  Sensorimotor (0–2), preoperational (2–7), concrete operational (7–11), formal operational (11+).

  **Answer:** Sensorimotor (0–2), preoperational (2–7), concrete operational (7–11), formal operational (11+).
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Piaget's ages are exact deadlines** The ages are approximate guides, and children vary hugely. Modern research also shows the stages overlap far more than Piaget claimed.
  - **Scaffolding means doing the task for the child** Scaffolding is temporary, adjustable support — hints and guidance — that is withdrawn as the child succeeds. Doing it for them builds dependence, not skill.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="What is object permanence, and in which stage does it develop?" hint="Out of sight...">
    Understanding that objects continue to exist when out of sight; it develops in the sensorimotor stage.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Contrast Piaget's and Vygotsky's views of how children learn." hint="Lone scientist versus social learner.">
    Piaget saw children as lone scientists constructing understanding through maturational stages; Vygotsky saw learning as fundamentally social, driven by interaction, culture and scaffolding within the ZPD.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Define the zone of proximal development." hint="It is a gap between two levels of performance.">
    The gap between what a child can do alone and what they can achieve with guidance from a more knowledgeable other.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="In Ainsworth's Strange Situation, how does a securely attached infant typically behave on reunion with the caregiver?" hint="Think of the secure base.">
    They seek comfort, are easily soothed, and then return to exploration — using the caregiver as a secure base.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
