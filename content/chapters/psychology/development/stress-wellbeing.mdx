## Stress and Wellbeing

Your heart pounds before an exam for the same reason your ancestor’s did before a predator: the fight-or-flight response. The exam won’t eat you — but your body doesn’t know that.

<Definition term="Fight-or-flight">
  **Fight-or-flight** is the sympathetic nervous system’s emergency mode: adrenaline and cortisol flood the body, raising heart rate, blood sugar and alertness — preparing you to fight or flee.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Acute stress: short bursts that can sharpen performance.
- Chronic stress: the alarm stuck on — linked to anxiety, depression, heart disease, weakened immunity.
- What works: sleep, exercise, social connection, reframing, planned breaks.
- What doesn’t: avoidance, doomscrolling, substances — they borrow calm from tomorrow.

<Example title="Exam stress, reframed">
  Studies show students who reinterpret butterflies as ‘my body preparing me to perform’ do better than those who try to calm down. Same arousal, different story.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Distinguish acute from chronic stress." hint="Alarm vs alarm stuck on.">
    Acute stress is a short-term response to an immediate challenge; chronic stress is prolonged activation that damages health.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Give two evidence-based ways to manage exam stress." hint="Body and mind.">
    Exercise/sleep for the body; reframing arousal as readiness, plus planned study breaks, for the mind.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Stress is an evolved alarm: useful briefly, harmful chronically.
  - Fight-or-flight mobilises the body via adrenaline and cortisol.
  - Sleep, exercise and connection are the best buffers.
  - How you interpret stress changes what it does to you.
</Summary>
