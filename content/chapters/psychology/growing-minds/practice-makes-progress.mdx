Your brain is not fixed like a stone — it is more like a muscle that grows stronger with exercise. In this chapter you will find out how practice, help and even mistakes build your learning power, and meet Carol Dweck's famous idea of the growth mindset.

This lesson focuses on Practice Makes Progress: see how trying again and again grows new skills, and learn why effort matters more than being perfect first time.

## Key ideas

### Practice rewires your brain

Every time you practise something, the connections in your brain for that skill get stronger — and that is why a song you play daily becomes easy. Nobody is born good at spellings or football; they get good by repeating, failing and trying again.

### Your self-talk matters

Dweck found that the words in your head shape how you learn. Thinking 'I can't do this' is a fixed-mindset thought that makes you quit. Adding one word — 'I can't do this yet' — turns it into a growth-mindset thought that keeps you trying. 'Yet' is one of the most powerful words in learning.

**Key term — effort:** Trying hard and keeping going, even when something is tricky — the fuel that helps your brain grow.

<WorkedExample title="From 'I can't' to 'I can't yet'">
  Imagine: Mia keeps getting her times tables wrong and says, 'I'm just bad at maths.' Her friend suggests a growth-mindset switch. What happens next?

  Spot the fixed-mindset thought: 'I'm just bad at maths.'.
  Add the magic word: 'I'm not good at times tables yet.'.
  Choose effort plus strategy: practise for five minutes every day, and learn them as a song.
  Ask for help: Mia asks her teacher for tips when she gets stuck.
  Track progress: after two weeks of practice, her scores have clearly improved.

  **Answer:** Mia's brain was never 'bad at maths' — it simply had not built those connections yet. With daily practice, a fun strategy and help when stuck, her scores improved, proving to her that ability grows with effort.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **A growth mindset means just trying harder at the same thing** Dweck's idea is about effort plus better strategies and help. Repeating what is not working without changing your approach is not a growth mindset.
  - **If you are clever, everything should be easy** Even the cleverest people find new things hard at first. Difficulty is a sign you are learning, not a sign you lack ability.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="What is a growth mindset?" hint="Think about what Carol Dweck says about abilities.">
    The belief that your abilities can improve through effort, good strategies and help from others.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Name two strategies Mia could use to improve her times tables." hint="Think beyond just trying harder.">
    Any two: practise a little every day, learn them as a song, use flashcards, ask a teacher for tips, practise with a friend.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="How is a fixed mindset different?" hint="Think of abilities as set in stone.">
    A fixed mindset is the belief that your abilities cannot change much, which can make people avoid challenges.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why do mistakes help your brain learn?" hint="Think of a mistake as a message.">
    Mistakes show the brain which approaches do not work, so it can try new ones — each mistake teaches something.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
