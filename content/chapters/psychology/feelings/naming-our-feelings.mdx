Feelings are like the weather inside your mind — they change all the time, and every one of them is normal. In this chapter you will learn the words for your emotions, find out what they are trying to tell you, and practise sharing them in kind, safe ways.

This lesson focuses on Naming Our Feelings: learn the words for happy, sad, angry, scared, surprised and calm, and practise spotting each feeling in yourself.

<Definition term="Naming Our Feelings">
  Learn the words for happy, sad, angry, scared, surprised and calm, and practise spotting each feeling in yourself.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### You can choose your actions

You cannot always choose your feelings, but you can choose what you do with them. Shouting or hitting hurts other people, while using words, taking a pause, or asking for help keeps everyone safe. Practising this choice is a skill that gets easier over time.

### Feelings are messages

Every feeling is trying to tell you something. Anger can mean something feels unfair, sadness can mean you need comfort, and nervousness can mean you want to be careful. When you listen to the message, you can decide what to do next instead of just reacting.

**Key term — body clue:** A signal in your body that shows a feeling, like a wobbly tummy when you are nervous or hot cheeks when you are angry.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Naming Our Feelings">
  Name three feelings and one body clue for each.

  Answers vary, but could include: happy → smiling and bouncy energy; nervous → wobbly tummy; angry → hot face and clenched fists.

  **Answer:** Answers vary, but could include: happy → smiling and bouncy energy; nervous → wobbly tummy; angry → hot face and clenched fists.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Feelings you hide will just go away** Hidden feelings usually grow bigger and pop out later. Naming a feeling out loud or drawing it helps it shrink.
  - **You should always cheer someone up straight away** Jumping to fix a feeling can make someone feel unheard. Listening first and showing you understand usually helps much more.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Why might hiding your feelings make things worse?" hint="Think about what happens to a feeling you never name.">
    Hidden feelings tend to grow and burst out later, often at the wrong moment. Naming them helps them settle.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="True or false: it is okay to feel angry sometimes." hint="Think about whether anger is a message or a misbehaviour.">
    True. Anger is a normal feeling; what matters is expressing it safely.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Name two calm-down tools you could use when a big feeling arrives." hint="Think of things that slow your body down.">
    Any two: slow breathing, counting to ten, drawing the feeling, squeezing a soft toy, asking a trusted adult for help.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is empathy?" hint="It starts with noticing how someone else feels.">
    Noticing how someone else feels and caring about it.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
