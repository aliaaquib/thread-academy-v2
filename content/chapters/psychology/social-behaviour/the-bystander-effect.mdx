## Key ideas

### Crowds can freeze helping

Darley and Latané were struck by the 1964 murder of Kitty Genovese in New York, after newspaper reports claimed witnesses failed to intervene. Their experiments revealed the bystander effect: the more people present, the less likely any one person is to help, largely because of diffusion of responsibility — everyone assumes someone else will act. Later reporting showed the original newspaper account exaggerated some details, but the laboratory effect itself has been replicated many times and remains a cornerstone of social psychology.

### Persuasion takes two routes

Petty and Cacioppo's elaboration likelihood model says persuasion works through a central route — careful thought about strong arguments — or a peripheral route — mental shortcuts like a celebrity's smile or a catchy slogan. The central route creates attitudes that last longer and resist change. Attitudes can also shift through cognitive dissonance: Festinger showed that when our actions clash with our beliefs, the resulting discomfort pushes us to change the belief to match the behaviour.

**Key term — diffusion of responsibility:** When people in a group each feel less personally responsible, so everyone assumes someone else will act.

<WorkedExample title="Why did nobody help?">
  Imagine: a student drops their books and falls on a busy school corridor as dozens of pupils walk past. Nobody stops. Use the bystander effect to explain why — and suggest how to break it.

  Dozens of bystanders mean diffusion of responsibility: each pupil assumes someone else will help.
  Pluralistic ignorance kicks in: everyone looks calm, so each person reads the calm faces as evidence that nothing is wrong.
  Evaluation apprehension adds fear of looking foolish by overreacting.
  To break it, the fallen student should single out one person: 'You in the blue jumper — please help me pick these up.'.
  This makes responsibility personal, and research shows the named individual will almost always help.

  **Answer:** Each passer-by assumes someone else will help and takes others' inaction as a cue that nothing is wrong. Pointing at one specific person removes the diffusion of responsibility and almost always gets help — a practical lesson straight from Darley and Latané's research.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **The bystander effect means people are selfish** It is not about selfishness but about social processes: diffusion of responsibility and uncertainty. In experiments, the very same people help readily when they are alone.
  - **Strong arguments always persuade** The elaboration likelihood model shows arguments only work through the central route when people are motivated and able to think carefully. Otherwise peripheral cues — who says it, how it looks — do the persuading.
</ImportantNote>
