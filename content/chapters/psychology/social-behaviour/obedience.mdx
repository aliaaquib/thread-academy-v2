## Obedience

After the Holocaust, the question burned: were the perpetrators monsters, or ordinary people in monstrous situations? Milgram tested it in a lab.

<Definition term="Agentic state">
  The **agentic state** is when a person sees themselves as an agent carrying out another’s wishes — shifting responsibility to the authority figure, which makes harmful obedience psychologically easier.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Milgram (1963): 65% of participants delivered the maximum 450V shock when ordered.
- Proximity matters: obedience fell when the ‘learner’ was in the same room.
- Legitimacy matters: obedience fell when the experimenter seemed less authoritative.
- Lesson: situations shape behaviour more than personality does — but that doesn’t excuse it.

<Example title="‘I was just following orders’">
  Participants sweated, trembled and protested — yet continued when the experimenter said ‘the experiment requires that you continue’. Distress plus obedience: the agentic state in action.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="What is the agentic state, and how does it explain Milgram’s results?" hint="Responsibility shifts.">
    Participants acted as the experimenter’s agents, feeling he was responsible — so they obeyed despite distress.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Give one criticism of Milgram’s study." hint="Ethics — or realism.">
    Ethics: severe deception and stress without true consent. Or: demand characteristics — participants may have guessed the shocks weren’t real.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - 65% obeyed to the maximum shock under authority.
  - Obedience depends on proximity and perceived legitimacy.
  - The agentic state shifts felt responsibility upward.
  - Situations powerfully shape — but don’t excuse — behaviour.
</Summary>
