</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Consonants that catch learners out

The j sounds like a strong English h (jamón = ha-MON). In most of Spain, z (and c before e or i) sounds like 'th' in 'think', while in Latin America it sounds like 's'. The double r needs a trill — flutter your tongue against the ridge behind your teeth; it comes with practice.

### Stress follows rules

Words ending in a vowel, n or s stress the second-to-last syllable (casa, hablan). Words ending in other consonants stress the last syllable (hotel, ciudad). A written accent breaks the rule and always marks the stress, as in teléfono and lápiz.

**Key term — erre:** The rolled or trilled r, as in perro (dog). A single r between vowels is just a quick tap, as in pero (but) — and the difference changes meaning.

<WorkedExample title="Reading 'mariposa'">
  Pronounce the word mariposa (butterfly) syllable by syllable.

  Split into syllables: ma-ri-po-sa.
  Give each vowel its pure sound: a, i, o, a.
  The single r between vowels is a quick tap.
  It ends in a vowel, so stress the second-to-last syllable: po.
  Say it smoothly: ma-ri-PO-sa.

  **Answer:** ma-ri-PO-sa — pure vowels, a tapped r, and stress on 'po'.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Giving up on the rolled r** Almost everyone can learn it with practice: start by saying 'butter' quickly and let the tongue flutter.
  - **Pronouncing vowels like English** Never say 'cay-sa' for casa — Spanish a is always 'ah', so it is CA-sa.
</ImportantNote>

  <PracticeItem question="How do you pronounce ñ?" hint="Think of the middle of 'canyon'.">

  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Where is the stress in 'mesa'?" hint="It ends in a vowel.">

  </PracticeItem>

  </PracticeItem>
