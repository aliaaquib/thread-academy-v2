Fields are physics' way of describing action at a distance. Gravity, electricity and magnetism all fill space with invisible influence — and all three can be mapped, measured and calculated. Welcome to the architecture of the universe.

This lesson focuses on Gravitational Fields: mapping g fields with F = GmM/r² and linking field strength to potential.

  Mapping g fields with F = GmM/r² and linking field strength to potential.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Fields are mapped with lines

Field lines show direction — the way a test mass or positive charge would be pushed — and spacing shows strength: closer lines mean a stronger field. Gravitational and electric field lines radiate from masses and charges; magnetic field lines form closed loops, emerging from north poles and entering south poles.

### Inverse-square laws govern point sources

Around a point mass, g = GM/r²; around a point charge, E = Q/(4πε₀r²). Doubling the distance quarters the field strength — the same mathematics in both cases, hinting at a deep unity. Inside a uniform field, like between charged plates, E = V/d is constant everywhere.

**Key term — field strength:** Force per unit mass (N/kg) or per unit charge (N/C) at a point in the field.

<WorkedExample title="Field between charged plates">
  Two parallel plates 0.05 m apart have a potential difference of 200 V. Calculate the electric field strength between them.

  For a uniform field, E = V/d.
  Substitute: E = 200 ÷ 0.05.
  200 ÷ 0.05 = 4000.

  **Answer:** 4000 V/m (equivalently 4000 N/C), directed towards the negative plate.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Confusing electric potential with field strength** Potential (V) is energy per unit charge; field strength (V/m) is its gradient — E = −ΔV/Δx.
  - **Drawing field lines crossing** Field lines never cross — a crossing would mean two field directions at one point, which is impossible.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="A 0.3 m wire carrying 2 A sits perpendicular to a 0.5 T field. Find the force on it." hint="F = BIl.">
    0.5 × 2 × 0.3 = 0.3 N.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Describe the electric field pattern around an isolated positive point charge." hint="Which way would a positive test charge be pushed?">
    Radial lines pointing straight outwards from the charge, getting further apart with distance as the field weakens.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Use Fleming's left-hand rule: magnetic field into the page, current to the right. Which way is the force?" hint="First finger field, second finger current.">
    Upwards — with the first finger pointing into the page and the second finger to the right, the thumb points up.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why is no work done moving a charge along an equipotential?" hint="What is the potential difference along it?">
    Potential is constant along an equipotential, so ΔV = 0 and W = QΔV = 0.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
