French numbers hide famous quirks — seventy is literally 'sixty-ten'! This chapter builds confident counting to 100, the essential colours, and how adjectives agree with nouns.

This lesson focuses on Numbers to Twenty: count from un to vingt.

<Definition term="Numbers to Twenty">
  Count from un to vingt.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### The logic of 70–99

After soixante-neuf comes soixante-dix (60+10), then 71 = soixante et onze. Eighty is quatre-vingts (4×20), losing its s when followed by another number: quatre-vingt-deux. Ninety is quatre-vingt-dix. Belgians and Swiss say septante, octante, nonante — simpler, but learn the French way first.

### Colour agreement

Adjectives match gender and number: un chat noir, une fleur noire, des chats noirs. Colours derived from nouns (orange, marron) usually do not change: des chaussures marron. But rose, mauve and pourpre are exceptions that do agree.

**Key term — quatre-vingts:** Eighty — 'four-twenties'. It loses its s when another number follows: quatre-vingt-trois (83); 90 is quatre-vingt-dix.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Numbers to Twenty">
  How do you say 83?

  Quatre-vingt-trois.

  **Answer:** Quatre-vingt-trois.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Keeping the s in 'quatre-vingt-trois'** Quatre-vingts (80) but quatre-vingt-trois (83) — the s drops when another number follows.
  - **Saying 'septante' in France** In France it is soixante-dix — septante is Belgian and Swiss usage.
</ImportantNote>
