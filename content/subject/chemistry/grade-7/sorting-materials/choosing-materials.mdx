</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Matching materials to jobs

People choose materials for their properties: windows are made of glass because it is transparent, and wellington boots are made of rubber because it is waterproof and bendy. A material that is perfect for one job can be wrong for another — glass windows let in light, but glass would be a poor choice for a football. Good designers always ask what the object needs to do first.

### Sorting by one property

When you sort materials, pick just one property at a time, for example magnetic or not magnetic. A fridge magnet sticks to steel but not to plastic, wood or glass, so steel goes in one group and the others go in another. Using one rule keeps the groups fair and makes the pattern easy to see.

**Key term — material:** Anything things are made from, such as wood, plastic, glass, fabric or metal.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Choosing Materials">
  Which material would be best for a window: wood, glass or fabric? Give a reason.

  Glass, because it is transparent so light can pass through and you can see through it.

  **Answer:** Glass, because it is transparent so light can pass through and you can see through it.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Saying a material is useless because it failed one test** Failing one test does not make a material useless. Fabric soaks up water, which is bad for a raincoat but perfect for a towel — the job decides which properties matter.
  - **Sorting by colour alone** Colour is a poor sorting rule because many different materials can be the same colour. Sort by a property that tells you something useful, like hardness or waterproofness.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Suggest a material for a playground slide and explain your choice using two properties." hint="Think about safety and weather.">
    Plastic is a good choice because it is smooth, so children slide easily, and waterproof, so rain does not damage it. Other sensible answers with two properties are acceptable.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="A raincoat is made from a special fabric. Which property makes it good for a raincoat?" hint="What must a raincoat stop?">
    It is waterproof, so rain cannot soak through it.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Name two properties you could test to sort a pile of different spoons." hint="Think about what a magnet or water could tell you.">
    Any two sensible properties, for example whether they are magnetic and whether they are waterproof (metal versus wood).
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="True or false: all metals are magnetic." hint="Test a magnet on a copper coin or an aluminium can in your head.">
    False. Iron and steel are magnetic, but metals such as copper and aluminium are not.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
