<Definition term="What Is a Liquid">
  See how liquids flow and take the shape of their container.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Liquids take the shape of their container

Pour water into a tall glass and it becomes tall and thin; pour the same water into a wide bowl and it becomes short and wide. The water flows because its shape is not fixed, but the amount of water stays the same. This is why we measure liquids by pouring them into measuring jugs.

### Solids keep their shape

A solid holds its shape wherever you put it: a wooden block is still a block in your hand, in a box or on the table. You can often change a solid's shape by squashing, bending or cutting it, but it will not flow into a new shape on its own. That is why buildings, furniture and toys are mostly made from solids.

**Key term — liquid:** A material that flows and takes the shape of its container, like water or milk.

<WorkedExample title="Is it a solid or a liquid?">
  You are given sand, honey, a marble and orange juice. Decide which are solids and which are liquids, and explain each choice.

  Ask of each material: does it keep its own shape, or does it flow?
  The marble keeps its shape wherever it is put: solid.
  The orange juice flows and takes the shape of its cup: liquid.
  Honey flows very slowly when poured: it is a liquid, even though it is thick.
  Sand is trickier: each grain is a solid, and the grains pour like a liquid — but every single grain keeps its own shape, so sand is made of solids.

  **Answer:** Solids: marble, sand (each grain keeps its shape). Liquids: orange juice, honey (both flow).
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Calling thick liquids solids** Honey, treacle and paint pour slowly, but they still flow and take the shape of their container. Thickness does not make something a solid — flowing does make it a liquid.
  - **Thinking melted ice has disappeared** When ice melts, it turns into water — the material is still there, just in a different state. If you freeze the water, the ice comes back, which proves nothing was lost.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Sand pours like a liquid. Explain why scientists still call each grain a solid." hint="Look closely at a single grain.">
    Each grain keeps its own shape and does not flow; only the pile of grains moves.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why is a chair a solid?" hint="What happens to its shape when you move it?">
    It keeps its own shape wherever you put it and does not flow.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Name one solid and one liquid you used today." hint="Think about breakfast or getting dressed.">
    Any sensible pair, for example toast (solid) and milk (liquid).
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Pour 100 ml of water from a beaker into a bottle. What changes and what stays the same?" hint="Think about shape and amount.">
    The shape changes to fit the bottle, but the amount (volume) of water stays the same.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
