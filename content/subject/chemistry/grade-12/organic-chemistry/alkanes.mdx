Carbon is the great connector: it bonds to itself in chains, rings and branches, giving millions of organic compounds. A handful of mechanisms — electrophilic addition, nucleophilic substitution, oxidation — explain how they transform. In this chapter you will meet the main families, draw the mechanisms and plan syntheses.

This lesson focuses on Meeting the Alkanes: study saturated hydrocarbons, fractional distillation and combustion.

## Key ideas

### Alcohols oxidise in stages

Primary alcohols oxidise first to aldehydes and then to carboxylic acids: ethanol → ethanal → ethanoic acid, using acidified potassium dichromate. Gentle distillation collects the aldehyde; reflux drives oxidation all the way to the acid. Secondary alcohols stop at ketones, and tertiary alcohols resist oxidation — a difference used to classify unknown alcohols.

### The double bond dominates alkene chemistry

Alkenes contain a C=C double bond: one sigma bond plus one pi bond, and the pi bond's exposed electron density attracts electrophiles. In electrophilic addition, the pi bond breaks and both carbons gain new atoms — for example, ethene + HBr → bromoethane. With unsymmetrical alkenes, Markownikoff's rule predicts the major product: the hydrogen adds to the carbon already carrying more hydrogens, giving the more stable carbocation.

**Key term — homologous series:** A family of compounds with the same functional group and a general formula, e.g. alkanes CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Meeting the Alkanes">
  Write the equation for the complete combustion of propane.

  C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O.

  **Answer:** C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Forgetting that alkanes only react by free-radical substitution** Alkanes are saturated and unreactive towards electrophiles — with halogens they react only in UV light by free-radical substitution. Writing an electrophilic addition for an alkane is a fundamental error.
  - **Drawing curly arrows starting at atoms** Curly arrows show electron pairs moving: they start at the electron source (a bond or lone pair) and point to where the electrons go. An arrow starting at an atom rather than the bond is meaningless.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Bromine water is added to ethene. Describe what you see and name the product." hint="What happens to the orange colour?">
    The orange bromine water decolourises; the product is 1,2-dibromoethane.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Explain why the addition of HBr to propene gives mainly 2-bromopropane." hint="Which carbocation is more stable?">
    H⁺ adds to C1, giving a secondary carbocation on C2, which is more stable than the primary alternative; Br⁻ then attacks C2 (Markownikoff's rule).
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Acidified potassium dichromate is refluxed with ethanol. Name the final organic product and describe the colour change." hint="Reflux drives oxidation to completion.">
    Ethanoic acid; the orange dichromate turns green as it is reduced.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Name the structural isomers of C₄H₁₀." hint="One straight chain, one branched.">
    Butane (CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃) and methylpropane (CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃).
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
