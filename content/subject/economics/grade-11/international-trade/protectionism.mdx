## Key ideas

### Protectionism versus free trade

Tariffs and quotas shield domestic jobs and infant industries and can protect national security, but they raise consumer prices, invite retaliation and shelter inefficiency. Globalisation has lifted millions from poverty through trade-led growth, yet created losers in declining industries — hence demands for retraining and compensation.

### The gains from trade

Ricardo showed that if Portugal gives up less wine to make cloth than England does, both gain by specialising and trading — even if Portugal is better at both. Trade enlarges both nations' consumption possibilities, though the gains may be unevenly shared within each country.

**Key term — tariff:** A tax on imports that raises their price, protecting domestic producers but hurting consumers.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Protectionism Explained">
  Give one argument for and one against tariffs.

  For: protects domestic jobs and infant industries. Against: raises consumer prices and risks retaliation.

  **Answer:** For: protects domestic jobs and infant industries. Against: raises consumer prices and risks retaliation.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Thinking exports are good and imports bad** Imports give consumers choice and lower prices and supply inputs for exporters — a surplus is not automatically better than a deficit.
  - **Confusing depreciation with devaluation** Depreciation is a market-driven fall in a floating currency; devaluation is a deliberate cut in a fixed exchange rate.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="What is a quota?" hint="A limit, not a tax.">
    A physical limit on the quantity of a good that may be imported.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="State Ricardo's principle of comparative advantage." hint="Think opportunity cost.">
    Countries gain by specialising in goods they produce at the lowest opportunity cost, then trading.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="If £1 = $1.27, how many dollars does £50 buy?" hint="Multiply.">
    $63.50 (50 × 1.27).
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="The pound depreciates. What happens to the price of UK exports abroad?" hint="Cheaper or dearer?">
    They become cheaper in foreign currency, boosting demand for them.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
