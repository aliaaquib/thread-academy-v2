Nobody is born knowing how to queue, share or say please: we learn it all. Socialisation is the lifelong process by which we learn our culture's norms, values and roles, turning biological infants into members of society. In this chapter you will meet the agents that teach us, compare the first lessons of childhood with later ones, and discover Mead's famous account of how the self itself is a social product.

This lesson focuses on Agents of Socialisation: meet the family, school, peers, media and religion as our social teachers.

</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Secondary socialisation never stops

From the first day of school, new agents take over part of the teaching: teachers and the hidden curriculum of rules and routines, peer groups with their fierce codes of cool, and the media with its flood of images and stories. Peer groups become especially powerful in the teenage years, sometimes pulling young people away from family values. Workplaces, partners and religions continue the process deep into adult life.

### Primary socialisation starts at home

In the early years the family teaches the essentials: language, table manners, right from wrong, and what it means to be a boy or a girl in that culture. Because these lessons come first and carry powerful emotions, they sink deepest and are hardest to undo. Sociologists regard the family as the most important agent of socialisation, though families differ greatly in what they teach.

**Key term — Agent of socialisation:** Any person, group or institution that teaches us norms and values, including family, school, peers, media and religion.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Agents of Socialisation">
  Name four agents of socialisation.

  Any four of: family, school, peer group, media, religion, workplace.

  **Answer:** Any four of: family, school, peer group, media, religion, workplace.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Thinking socialisation finishes at eighteen** Socialisation is lifelong: new jobs, partnerships, parenthood and retirement all require learning new roles.
  - **Assuming the media has no real effect** The media is a major secondary agent, shaping ideas about beauty, success and normality, especially for young people.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="What is the difference between primary and secondary socialisation?" hint="Think first lessons versus later lessons.">
    Primary socialisation is the first, intense learning of basic norms and values in the family; secondary socialisation is later learning from school, peers, media and others.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="A teenager starts dressing like her new friends and arguing with her parents' views. Which agent is at work, and is this primary or secondary socialisation?" hint="Which group becomes powerful in the teens?">
    The peer group, and this is secondary socialisation, which can challenge primary lessons from the family.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="An adult moves abroad for work and must learn new greetings, food customs and workplace norms. What does this show about socialisation?" hint="Remember the concept about lifelong learning.">
    It shows socialisation continues throughout life: adults learn new roles and norms whenever they enter new cultures or situations.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Give one example of the hidden curriculum at school." hint="It is taught without being in any textbook.">
    Any valid example, e.g. learning punctuality, obedience to authority, competition, or gendered expectations through routines and teacher behaviour.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
