## Key ideas

### Weber: three dimensions of inequality

Max Weber agreed that economics matters but argued stratification has three separate dimensions. Class is a person's market situation, their income and wealth prospects. Status is social honour or prestige, which does not always match wealth. Party is power: the ability to get one's way through organisations and politics. Together they shape a person's life chances, the opportunities they have to get on in life. Weber's model explains puzzles Marx cannot, such as the high status of a low-paid nurse.

### Marx: two great classes

Karl Marx argued that capitalist society is split between the bourgeoisie, who own the means of production such as factories and land, and the proletariat, who own only their labour and must sell it to survive. Because employers profit by paying workers less than the value they create, Marx saw exploitation and class conflict as built into capitalism, predicting it would end in revolution. Critics note the growth of a large middle class and that revolution did not arrive in advanced capitalist societies as he expected.

**Key term — Glass ceiling:** The invisible barrier of attitudes and practices that stops women and minorities reaching the top jobs, despite their qualifications.

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Claiming the gender pay gap means women are illegally paid less for identical work** Equal pay for equal work is required by law, for example the Equality Act 2010 in the UK; the pay gap is an average across different jobs, hours and career breaks, which is why sociologists explain it through occupational segregation, part-time work and the glass ceiling.
  - **Saying Marx and Weber agreed about stratification** They differ fundamentally: Marx saw two classes defined by ownership driving history towards revolution, while Weber saw three dimensions of class, status and party shaping life chances.
</ImportantNote>
