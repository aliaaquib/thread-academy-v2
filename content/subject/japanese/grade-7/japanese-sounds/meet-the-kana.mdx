Japanese is written with sounds you can see. Every kana character stands for one whole syllable, like か (ka) or み (mi), so once you learn the shapes you can read real Japanese words aloud. In this chapter you will meet both kana scripts and learn the clever marks that multiply their sounds.

This lesson focuses on Meet the Kana: discover the two Japanese syllable scripts and what each one is for.

## Key ideas

### Hiragana for Japanese words, katakana for borrowed words

Hiragana writes native words like ねこ (neko, cat) and the grammar bits attached to verbs. Katakana writes words borrowed from other languages, such as パン (pan, bread) and アイス (aisu, ice cream). If a word sounds foreign, reach for katakana first — that single habit will guide most of your script choices.

### One kana equals one syllable

English letters spell sounds piece by piece, but each kana is a complete syllable. The word さくら (sakura, cherry blossom) is just three kana: さ (sa), く (ku) and ら (ra). There are 46 basic hiragana, arranged in the traditional gojūon (fifty-sounds) order that starts あいうえお (a i u e o). Learn the rows in order and new characters slot neatly into place.

**Key term — ひらがな (hiragana):** One of Japan's two kana scripts. Its rounded characters are used for native Japanese words and grammar endings, such as the べます in たべます (tabemasu, to eat).

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Meet the Kana">
  Which script would you use to write the English loanword 'computer'?

  Katakana: コンピューター (konpyūtā).

  **Answer:** Katakana: コンピューター (konpyūtā).
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Mixing up シ (shi) and ツ (tsu) in katakana** These two look almost identical. シ has its strokes slanting the same way, like a smile; ツ has more upright strokes, like a frown. Compare them side by side until the difference sticks.
  - **Reading が (ga) as か (ka)** The dakuten ゛ is small but mighty. がっこう (gakkō) means school, while かっこう (kakkō) means appearance — always check for the two little strokes.
</ImportantNote>
