Historians cannot visit the past, so they work like detectives with the clues left behind: letters, photographs, newspapers, buildings. But clues can mislead. This chapter teaches you to ask where a source comes from, why it was made, and how far you can trust it.

This lesson focuses on What Counts as Evidence: primary and secondary sources, and why historians need both.

</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Strong answers weigh sources

In exams, top answers do more than describe sources: they compare them, note agreements and disagreements, and judge usefulness using provenance. Saying 'Source A is useful because it is a primary source' is weak; saying 'Source A is useful for showing X, but limited because...' is strong.

### Every source has a maker

A source was created by a real person with reasons of their own: to persuade, to record, to celebrate, to complain. A government poster and a soldier's diary describe the same war very differently because their purposes differ. Always ask: who made this, and why?

**Key term — Secondary source:** An account made later, using primary sources — for example, a modern textbook about the Victorians.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: What Counts as Evidence">
  What is the difference between a primary and a secondary source?

  A primary source comes from the time studied; a secondary source is made later, using primary sources.

  **Answer:** A primary source comes from the time studied; a secondary source is made later, using primary sources.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Calling every old source a primary source** A primary source must come from the time studied. A 1990s documentary about the Victorians is secondary, however old it looks.
  - **Writing 'the source is biased so it is useless'** Bias does not make a source useless. Explain what the bias reveals and what the source cannot show, instead of dismissing it.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="A suffragette newspaper supports votes for women. Is it useless to a historian?" hint="Bias can be a clue.">
    No — it is excellent evidence of what suffragettes believed and how they argued, though one-sided on the wider debate.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Improve this sentence: 'Source A is useful because it is primary.'" hint="Say what it shows and what limits it.">
    For example: 'Source A is useful for showing soldiers' daily conditions because it is a diary written at the front, but limited because one soldier's experience may not be typical.'.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What does provenance mean?" hint="It is about the source's origins.">
    Who made the source, when and where — its origin.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Give one reason a source might be biased." hint="The maker may want to persuade.">
    It was made to persuade people to one side, e.g. propaganda or a campaign leaflet.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
