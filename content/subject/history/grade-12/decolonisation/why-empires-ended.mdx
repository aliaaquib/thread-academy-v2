Between 1945 and the 1970s, dozens of new nations won independence as European empires collapsed. Decolonisation was driven by determined nationalist movements — and accelerated by the Second World War's destruction of imperial power. This chapter examines why empires fell, how independence was won, and what came after.

This lesson focuses on Why Empires Ended: war, debt and nationalism: the forces that broke up the European empires.

<Definition term="Why Empires Ended">
  War, debt and nationalism: the forces that broke up the European empires.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### The Second World War broke the empires

The war bankrupted Britain, shattered the myth of European invincibility — Japan's 1942 capture of Singapore stunned the British Empire — and armed colonial soldiers who returned home demanding rights. Empires that survived the war could not afford, politically or financially, to keep their colonies by force.

### Freedom brought new challenges

New nations inherited borders drawn by empires, dividing peoples and sowing conflict — partition in 1947 caused massive displacement and violence. Yet independence also brought pride, democracy movements and new voices in world affairs. Historians debate whether decolonisation was a triumph, a tragedy, or — most often — both.

**Key term — Nationalism:** The belief that a people should govern themselves as an independent nation. Anti-colonial nationalism drove independence movements.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Why Empires Ended">
  Name two factors that weakened European empires after 1945.

  The financial ruin of the Second World War and the spread of anti-colonial nationalism (also: superpower pressure, the Atlantic Charter's promise of self-determination).

  **Answer:** The financial ruin of the Second World War and the spread of anti-colonial nationalism (also: superpower pressure, the Atlantic Charter's promise of self-determination).
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Saying Britain simply 'gave' independence** Independence was won through sustained pressure and struggle. 'Granting' independence misdescribes who drove the process.
  - **Treating partition as an afterthought** Partition was central to 1947: it created two states and caused enormous displacement and violence. Any account of Indian independence must confront it.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Who was Kwame Nkrumah?" hint="Think Ghana, 1957.">
    The nationalist leader who led Ghana (the Gold Coast) to independence in 1957, the first sub-Saharan African colony to do so.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why did imperial borders cause problems after independence?" hint="Empires drew lines ignoring local peoples.">
    Borders divided ethnic groups and forced rival peoples together, sowing conflict in new states.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is decolonisation?" hint="Think colonies becoming independent.">
    The process by which colonies gained independence from imperial rule.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="In which year did India gain independence, and what was partition?" hint="The year is 1947.">
    1947; partition was the division of British India into independent India and Pakistan.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
