</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Discussion is a team sport

Good discussions need everyone. Invite quieter members in with 'what do you think?', disagree with ideas rather than people, and summarise to check agreement: 'so we all agree that…'.

### Plan before you speak

Even a short talk needs a plan: one clear opening line, two or three main points in order, and a strong final sentence. A plan stops you rambling and gives you confidence when nerves strike.

**Key term — audience:** The people listening to you. You choose simpler words for younger children and more formal language for teachers or judges.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Group Discussion">
  How do you disagree politely in a discussion?

  Disagree with the idea, not the person — for example, I see it differently because….

  **Answer:** Disagree with the idea, not the person — for example, I see it differently because….
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Reading every word from notes** Notes should be bullet points, not a script. Reading aloud sounds flat and stops you looking at your audience.
  - **Speaking too fast when nervous** Nerves speed everyone up. Practise pausing — it feels long to you but sounds confident to listeners.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="How can you include a quiet person in a discussion?" hint="Ask them directly.">
    Invite them in by name, for example: What do you think, Sam?
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is your audience?" hint="Who is listening?">
    The people you are speaking to.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Name two discourse markers you could use in a talk." hint="Think firstly...">
    Any two of: firstly, in addition, however, in conclusion.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why should you slow down for your most important point?" hint="Think about the listener.">
    So the listener has time to absorb it and recognises that it matters.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
