Every text is a set of choices: this word not that one, this sentence shape not another. Language analysis — stylistics — gives you the technical vocabulary to name those choices and explain their effects with precision. It is the engine room of A-level English Language.

This lesson focuses on Meaning and Semantics: explore how words carry denotation, connotation and ambiguity.

<Definition term="Meaning and Semantics">

</Definition>

## Key ideas

### What is unsaid matters

Pragmatics studies implied meaning. If someone says It's cold in here, they usually mean close the window — the intended meaning differs from the literal one. Grice's cooperative principle suggests speakers aim to be informative, truthful, relevant and clear; when writers flout these maxims, irony and implicature are born.

### Lexical fields build worlds

Writers cluster related words — a lexical field — to create an atmosphere. A horror passage might draw on darkness, cold, silence and decay, with every word reinforcing the others. Spotting the field lets you describe a text's overall effect in one precise move.

**Key term — semantics:** The study of meaning in language — what words and sentences denote, connote and imply.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Meaning and Semantics">
  What is the difference between semantics and pragmatics?

  Semantics studies literal meaning; pragmatics studies meaning in context, including what is implied.

  **Answer:** Semantics studies literal meaning; pragmatics studies meaning in context, including what is implied.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Labelling without explaining** Naming a semantic field or low modality is not analysis. Always connect the label to its effect on the reader.
  - **Ignoring the bigger picture** Individual word choices matter, but check how they combine: a full commentary links lexis, grammar and pragmatics into one argument.
</ImportantNote>
