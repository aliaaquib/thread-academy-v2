## Key ideas

### Devices are tools, not decoration

Anaphora creates rhythm; the tricolon (a three-part list) feels complete and memorable; a rhetorical question invites the audience to agree without answering. Devices work because they match how brains process language — pattern, repetition and rhythm make ideas stick.

### The rhetorical triangle

Every persuasive act balances speaker, audience and message. Ethos convinces the audience to trust you, pathos makes them feel, and logos makes them think. The most powerful persuasion blends all three: evidence alone rarely moves people, and emotion alone rarely convinces them.

**Key term — anaphora:** Repeating a word or phrase at the start of successive clauses — as in Churchill's 'we shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight…' — to build rhythm and emotional force.

<WorkedExample title="Analyse a persuasive paragraph">
  Identify the rhetorical techniques in this paragraph and explain their effect: Our school library is closing, and with it a door to a thousand worlds. Who will lend a curious child their first adventure? We must act now — sign the petition, join the protest, save our library.

  Pathos: a door to a thousand worlds uses emotive metaphor — losing the library means losing imagination itself.
  Rhetorical question: Who will lend a curious child their first adventure? makes the reader supply the answer — nobody — which feels unacceptable.
  Tricolon: sign the petition, join the protest, save our library gives three clear actions in a memorable rhythm.
  Logos: the argument assumes libraries matter for children's learning — the emotional case carries an unstated logical one.
  Conclude: pathos opens the heart, the question engages the mind, and the tricolon drives action.

  **Answer:** The paragraph blends pathos (the thousand worlds metaphor), a rhetorical question that forces agreement, and a tricolon of imperatives that channels feeling into action.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Piling on rhetorical questions** One well-placed rhetorical question is powerful; five in a row sound hectoring. Vary your devices.
  - **Confusing anaphora with any repetition** Anaphora is repetition at the start of successive clauses — we shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight… — not just repeating a word anywhere.
</ImportantNote>
