Rhetoric is the art of persuasion, refined over two thousand years from Aristotle's Athens to today's debating chambers. Whether you are writing an essay, delivering a speech or simply winning an argument, rhetoric gives you the tools to move minds.

This lesson focuses on Ethos, Pathos, Logos: master Aristotle's three modes of persuasion.

  Master Aristotle's three modes of persuasion.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### The rhetorical triangle

Every persuasive act balances speaker, audience and message. Ethos convinces the audience to trust you, pathos makes them feel, and logos makes them think. The most powerful persuasion blends all three: evidence alone rarely moves people, and emotion alone rarely convinces them.

### Devices are tools, not decoration

Anaphora creates rhythm; the tricolon (a three-part list) feels complete and memorable; a rhetorical question invites the audience to agree without answering. Devices work because they match how brains process language — pattern, repetition and rhythm make ideas stick.

**Key term — ethos:** Persuasion through character and credibility. A speaker establishes ethos by showing expertise, honesty or shared values.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Ethos, Pathos, Logos">
  Name Aristotle's three modes of persuasion.

  Ethos, pathos and logos.

  **Answer:** Ethos, pathos and logos.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Pathos without logos** Emotion moves audiences but evidence convinces them. Even the most stirring speech needs facts beneath the feeling.
  - **Piling on rhetorical questions** One well-placed rhetorical question is powerful; five in a row sound hectoring. Vary your devices.
</ImportantNote>
