<Definition term="Efficiency and Friction">

</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Friction always takes its cut

Moving parts rub, and rubbing turns useful energy into heat and sound. Efficiency measures what fraction of the input becomes useful output, and friction keeps every real machine below 100 per cent. Engineers fight friction with lubrication, smooth surfaces and ball bearings, which replace sliding with rolling.

### Machines multiply force, not energy

A machine lets a small effort balance a large load, and the ratio of load to effort is its mechanical advantage. But the effort must move farther than the load moves, so the work in equals the work out, minus losses. No machine can output more energy than is put in: that would break the conservation of energy.

**Key term — Efficiency:** The percentage of input energy a machine turns into useful output: useful output divided by total input, times 100.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Efficiency and Friction">
  A ball bearing reduces friction in a wheel. How does this change the machine's efficiency?

  Efficiency rises, because less input energy is wasted as heat, leaving more as useful output.

  **Answer:** Efficiency rises, because less input energy is wasted as heat, leaving more as useful output.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Mixing up mechanical advantage and efficiency** Mechanical advantage compares forces; efficiency compares energies. A machine can have a huge mechanical advantage yet poor efficiency if friction wastes much of the input.
  - **Claiming a machine creates energy** Machines only transform energy and trade force for distance. Useful output energy can never exceed input energy.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Why can no machine be 100 per cent efficient?" hint="What happens where parts rub?">
    Friction always converts some input energy into wasted heat and sound.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="A motor takes in 400 J of electrical energy and delivers 300 J of useful work. What is its efficiency?" hint="Divide useful output by input, then multiply by 100.">
    75 per cent, because 300 divided by 400 = 0.75, times 100.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="A machine lifts an 800 N load with a 200 N effort. What is its mechanical advantage?" hint="Divide load by effort.">
    4, because 800 divided by 200 = 4.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="A lever has an effort arm of 2 m and a load arm of 0.5 m. An effort of 60 N is applied. What load is balanced?" hint="Balance the moments: 60 times 2 against load times 0.5.">
    240 N, because 60 times 2 = 120 N m, and 120 divided by 0.5 = 240.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
