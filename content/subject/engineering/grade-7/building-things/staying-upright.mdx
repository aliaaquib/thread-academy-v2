Engineers build towers, bridges and houses that must stay standing whatever the weather throws at them. Every one of those structures started life as someone's idea made real with simple materials. In this chapter you will design and build small structures of your own, and discover what keeps them standing.

This lesson focuses on Staying Upright: wide bases and low centres of mass keep towers from toppling.

<Definition term="Staying Upright">
  Wide bases and low centres of mass keep towers from toppling.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Wide bases stop toppling

A structure stays upright while its centre of mass hangs over its base. Making the base wider, or keeping heavy parts low down, gives the structure more room to lean before that point moves past the edge and it tips over. That is why towers are built broad at the bottom and narrow at the top.

### Triangles keep frames stiff

A square made of four sticks can squash sideways into a diamond because its corners can pivot. Adding a fifth stick across a corner turns the square into two triangles, and triangles cannot change shape without stretching a side, so the frame locks solid. Engineers call this bracing.

**Key term — Centre of mass:** The point where the whole weight of an object seems to act. An object topples when this point moves beyond the edge of its base.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Staying Upright">
  Why does a wide base make a tower more stable?

  A wide base keeps the tower's centre of mass over the base even when it leans, so it does not topple.

  **Answer:** A wide base keeps the tower's centre of mass over the base even when it leans, so it does not topple.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Building a tall, narrow tower** A tower that is tall and narrow topples easily because its centre of mass is high and its base is small. Widen the base and keep the heavy parts low.
  - **Forgetting to brace the squares** A frame of plain squares can lean sideways and collapse like a deck of cards. Add diagonal struts to make triangles.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Where should you put the heaviest parts of a tower, and why?" hint="A high centre of mass makes toppling easier.">
    Low down, because that keeps the centre of mass near the ground and the tower harder to tip over.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="A pyramid shape and a tall thin column have the same height. Which is more stable?" hint="Compare the width of their bases.">
    The pyramid, because its wide base gives the centre of mass more room before it passes the edge.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Your tower's middle joint keeps bending when loaded. What does this tell you?" hint="Failure points show you something useful.">
    The middle joint is the weakest point, so the design needs strengthening there, for example with an extra brace or a doubled tube.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="You build a square frame from four straws and it wobbles. What one change will stiffen it?" hint="Which shape cannot be squashed sideways?">
    Add a diagonal straw to make triangles; triangles cannot change shape without stretching a side.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
