## Key ideas

### The immune response

The body's first defences are barriers: skin, mucus, and hydrochloric acid in the stomach. If pathogens get through, white blood cells called phagocytes engulf them, while lymphocytes produce specific antibodies. After the infection, memory lymphocytes remain, so a second encounter triggers a faster, stronger response — this is the basis of natural immunity.

### Types of pathogen

Bacteria are single-celled organisms; some cause disease by releasing toxins. Viruses are far smaller and can only reproduce inside living cells, damaging them. Fungi such as athlete's foot thrive on skin, while protists such as the malaria parasite are spread by vectors like mosquitoes. Knowing the type matters because treatments differ: antibiotics kill bacteria but do nothing against viruses.

**Key term — antibody:** A Y-shaped protein made by white blood cells that locks onto a specific antigen on a pathogen, marking it for destruction.

  **Answer:** Any two of: intact skin; mucus trapping microbes; hydrochloric acid in the stomach killing microbes; hairs and mucus in the nose.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Taking antibiotics for a cold or flu** Colds and flu are caused by viruses, and antibiotics only kill bacteria. Using them unnecessarily also encourages antibiotic resistance.
  - **Thinking vaccines give you the disease** Vaccines contain dead, weakened or partial pathogens that cannot cause the disease; any mild symptoms come from the immune response itself.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Describe what a phagocyte does during an infection." hint="Think about engulfing.">
    A phagocyte is a white blood cell that engulfs pathogens and digests them with enzymes, destroying the invaders.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why do antibiotics not work against viruses?" hint="What do antibiotics target?">
    Antibiotics target structures and processes found in bacterial cells, such as cell walls. Viruses lack these structures and reproduce inside human cells, so antibiotics cannot attack them.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Explain how a vaccine gives long-term protection." hint="What cells remain afterwards?">
    The vaccine triggers an immune response that produces memory lymphocytes. If the real pathogen later invades, these memory cells mount a rapid response before the person becomes ill.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Name the four main types of pathogen and give one disease caused by each." hint="Bacteria, viruses, fungi and protists.">
    Bacteria – e.g. food poisoning or tuberculosis; viruses – e.g. flu or measles; fungi – e.g. athlete's foot; protists – e.g. malaria.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
