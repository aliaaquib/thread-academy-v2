## Key ideas

### Food webs and interdependence

A food web links many food chains, showing that most organisms eat, and are eaten by, several species. Removing one species ripples through the web: if rabbits decline, foxes may starve while grass flourishes. Decomposers complete the picture by breaking down dead organisms and waste, recycling nutrients back to producers.

### Pyramids of biomass and energy loss

A pyramid of biomass shows the dry mass at each trophic level, and it is almost always pyramid-shaped because biomass is lost between levels. Only about 10% of energy is transferred: the rest is lost through respiration and movement, in uneaten parts, and in waste such as faeces and urine. This is why food chains rarely have more than five trophic levels — there is too little energy left.

**Key term — trophic level:** A feeding level in a food chain or web, such as producer, primary consumer or secondary consumer.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Food Webs">
  In the food chain grass → rabbit → fox → flea, identify the producer, a primary consumer and a secondary consumer.

  Producer: grass. Primary consumer: rabbit. Secondary consumer: fox (the flea is a tertiary consumer).

  **Answer:** Producer: grass. Primary consumer: rabbit. Secondary consumer: fox (the flea is a tertiary consumer).
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Saying decomposers are not needed** Without decomposers, nutrients would stay locked in dead bodies and waste. Decomposers recycle them into the soil for producers to reuse.
  - **Drawing a pyramid of biomass with the widest bar at the top** Biomass decreases up the chain because energy is lost at each transfer, so the producer bar at the bottom is always the widest.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Explain why a food web gives a better picture than a single food chain." hint="How many food sources does a fox have?">
    Most organisms eat several different species and are eaten by several predators, so a web shows the true feeding relationships and how species depend on one another.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why do food chains rarely have more than five trophic levels?" hint="How much energy reaches the top?">
    Only about 10% of energy transfers at each level, so after several transfers too little energy remains to support another trophic level.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Describe two processes that return carbon dioxide to the atmosphere." hint="One involves living things breathing; the other involves burning.">
    Any two of: respiration by plants, animals and decomposers; combustion of fossil fuels or wood; decay releasing carbon dioxide.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Give three ways energy is lost between trophic levels." hint="Think about what the animal does and leaves behind.">
    Any three of: respiration (released as heat); movement; uneaten parts such as roots or bones; waste products such as faeces and urine.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
