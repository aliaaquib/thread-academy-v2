## Key ideas

### Citizenship is legal membership with a personal meaning

Legally, citizenship gives rights such as voting and consular protection, and it can be gained by birth or through naturalisation. Personally, it shapes identity and belonging: people can feel British, Welsh, Scottish or English in overlapping ways. The legal status and the sense of belonging do not always line up neatly, which is why citizenship can be debated.

### Rights and duties support each other

Citizens hold civil, political and social rights, from free speech to education and healthcare. These rights depend on duties: paying taxes funds public services, obeying the law keeps society orderly, and respecting others' rights protects everyone's freedom. A right claimed without regard for others' rights quickly breaks down.

**Key term — citizen:** A legal member of a state who holds rights such as voting and protection abroad, and owes duties such as obeying the law. Citizenship may be gained by birth or through naturalisation.

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Reducing citizenship to holding a passport** A passport is evidence of citizenship, not its substance. Citizenship involves legal rights, duties such as obeying the law, and participation in community life. Someone can hold the document yet take no part in civic life, or contribute greatly while waiting for formal status.
  - **Treating rights as unlimited** Rights such as free speech protect citizens from state overreach, but they are balanced against other people's rights and public safety. Courts and laws set boundaries, for example around incitement to violence. Understanding limits is part of understanding rights.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Distinguish citizenship from national identity." hint="Separate legal membership from cultural feeling.">
    Citizenship and nationality overlap but nationality often refers to legal membership of a state while identity can be cultural or regional. Someone may feel Scottish or Welsh culturally while holding British citizenship legally. The distinction helps explain debates about belonging.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is naturalisation, and why does it matter?" hint="Think of the path from resident to citizen.">
    Naturalisation is the legal process by which a non-citizen becomes a citizen, usually after residence, language and knowledge requirements. It matters because it turns long-term residents into full members with voting rights and full legal protection.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Distinguish civil, political and social rights." hint="Think freedoms, participation and welfare.">
    Civil rights protect freedoms such as speech and fair treatment, political rights cover voting and standing for office, and social rights cover access to education, healthcare and welfare. Together they let people live freely, take part in politics and meet basic needs.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Explain how civic duties support citizens' rights." hint="Link each duty to a right it protects.">
    Duties such as paying taxes, obeying the law, serving on juries and respecting others' rights make rights sustainable. Without taxation there is no funding for schools or courts; without law-abiding behaviour, freedoms cannot be enjoyed safely.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
