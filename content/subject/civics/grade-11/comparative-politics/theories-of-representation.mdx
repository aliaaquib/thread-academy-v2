## Key ideas

### Electoral systems shape party systems

The way votes are translated into seats influences how many parties thrive. First-past-the-post tends to favour two large parties, a pattern summarised as Duverger's law, because voters avoid wasting votes on smaller parties. Proportional representation lowers the barrier for smaller parties and often produces coalitions. Neither system is neutral: each rewards different strategies and different kinds of representation.

### Democratic theory asks who really governs

Pluralist theory sees power spread among competing groups, while elite theory argues that a small minority dominates even in democracies. Participatory theory holds that democracy is only healthy when citizens take part beyond voting. These theories lead to different tests of democratic quality: fair competition, real influence for ordinary people, or broad participation. A-level analysis should apply more than one lens to the same case.

**Key term — authoritarianism:** Rule in which political power is concentrated and opposition is restricted or banned, with limited scope for dissent and free political competition. It covers varied forms including military juntas and one-party states.

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Claiming proportional representation is always fairer** PR represents vote shares more accurately, but fairness has several dimensions. FPTP usually gives a clearer link between a representative and a local constituency and tends to produce decisive single-party government. The better system depends on which democratic values you prioritise.
  - **Treating all authoritarian regimes as the same** Authoritarian systems vary greatly: military juntas, one-party states, monarchies and personalist dictatorships organise power differently. Some allow limited opposition parties or local elections while blocking any real challenge nationally. Careful comparison needs sub-types, not a single label.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Analyse two likely effects of switching a country from FPTP to proportional representation." hint="Think about party numbers and how governments are formed.">
    PR usually increases the number of parties in parliament and makes single-party majorities rarer, so coalition or minority government becomes normal. Small parties gain seats and bargaining power, and voters can support them without wasting votes, but forming a government takes negotiation.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Explain Duverger's law and one limitation of it." hint="Name the mechanism, then note its limits.">
    It states that first-past-the-post tends to produce two-party systems because voters avoid wasting votes on smaller parties and parties merge to become viable winners. It is a tendency, not an iron law: regional parties and strong social cleavages can sustain more parties under FPTP.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Compare pluralist, elite and participatory theories of democracy." hint="Give each theory's answer to who governs.">
    Pluralists see power dispersed among competing interest groups with no single permanent winner. Elite theorists argue that a cohesive minority dominates key decisions even in democracies. Participatory theorists judge democracy by how far ordinary citizens actually take part beyond voting.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Distinguish presidential and parliamentary systems." hint="Focus on how the head of government gets and keeps the job.">
    A presidential system elects the head of government separately from the legislature for a fixed term, while a parliamentary system makes the prime minister dependent on the legislature's confidence. Presidential systems risk deadlock between separately elected branches; parliamentary systems fuse executive and legislature but allow governments to fall on confidence votes.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
