## Key ideas

### Electoral systems shape party systems

The way votes are translated into seats influences how many parties thrive. First-past-the-post tends to favour two large parties, a pattern summarised as Duverger's law, because voters avoid wasting votes on smaller parties. Proportional representation lowers the barrier for smaller parties and often produces coalitions. Neither system is neutral: each rewards different strategies and different kinds of representation.

### Institutions shape political behaviour

Whether a country has a presidential or parliamentary system affects how power is concentrated and checked. Presidential systems separate the executive from the legislature by direct election, while parliamentary systems fuse them through the cabinet's dependence on the legislature's confidence. Federal systems divide power between national and regional governments; unitary systems concentrate it nationally. Comparing these arrangements explains why similar policies can produce different political conflicts in different countries.

**Key term — regime:** The set of rules and norms that determine how political power is gained and exercised, such as democratic, authoritarian or hybrid arrangements. It is broader than a single government, which is the particular group in office.

<WorkedExample title="Predicting Party Systems from Electoral Rules">
  Two new democracies hold elections. Country A uses first-past-the-post in single-member constituencies. Country B uses proportional representation with a low threshold. Predict how their party systems are likely to differ after several elections, and explain why.

  Step 1: Recall Duverger's law: FPTP tends to produce two-party competition because voters fear wasting votes on candidates who cannot win the constituency.
  Step 2: Apply it to Country A: small parties struggle to win seats, so politics consolidates around two large parties and usually single-party government.
  Step 3: Apply PR logic to Country B: parties win seats roughly in proportion to votes, so smaller parties survive and enter parliament.
  Step 4: Consider governing consequences: Country B will often need coalitions, giving small parties bargaining power.
  Step 5: Note the trade-off: Country A gains decisive government but weaker representation of minorities; Country B gains fairer representation but more complex coalition politics.

  **Answer:** The FPTP country is likely to develop two dominant parties with single-party governments, while the PR country is likely to develop several parties and coalition governments. FPTP punishes small parties whose votes are spread thinly, encouraging voters to back one of two frontrunners; PR lets smaller parties win seats in proportion to their votes, so more viewpoints gain representation but governing requires negotiation between parties.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Claiming proportional representation is always fairer** PR represents vote shares more accurately, but fairness has several dimensions. FPTP usually gives a clearer link between a representative and a local constituency and tends to produce decisive single-party government. The better system depends on which democratic values you prioritise.
  - **Assuming democracy means elections and nothing else** Elections are necessary but not sufficient for liberal democracy, which also requires the rule of law, free speech, independent courts and protection of minorities. Regimes can hold elections while harassing opponents and controlling the media. Always check the conditions around the vote, not just the vote itself.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Analyse two likely effects of switching a country from FPTP to proportional representation." hint="Think about party numbers and how governments are formed.">
    PR usually increases the number of parties in parliament and makes single-party majorities rarer, so coalition or minority government becomes normal. Small parties gain seats and bargaining power, and voters can support them without wasting votes, but forming a government takes negotiation.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Explain Duverger's law and one limitation of it." hint="Name the mechanism, then note its limits.">
    It states that first-past-the-post tends to produce two-party systems because voters avoid wasting votes on smaller parties and parties merge to become viable winners. It is a tendency, not an iron law: regional parties and strong social cleavages can sustain more parties under FPTP.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Distinguish delegate, trustee and party models of representation." hint="Three models, three loyalties.">
    In the delegate model the representative follows voters' instructions; in the trustee model the representative uses independent judgement for the public good; in the party model the representative follows the party programme. Each answers differently who the representative ultimately serves: voters, conscience or party.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Define a hybrid regime and explain why political scientists treat it as a separate category." hint="Define it by the mixture, then justify the separate label.">
    A hybrid regime mixes democratic and authoritarian features, for example holding elections while restricting media freedom and harassing opponents. It is a distinct category because calling it either a democracy or a dictatorship would miss how it actually works: competition exists but on an uneven playing field.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
