Some of the most important changes in modern history were won by ordinary people demanding their rights. This chapter studies the great civil rights movements, from votes for women in Britain to the struggle against racial segregation in the United States and apartheid in South Africa. You will learn how disciplined campaigns, law and public opinion combined to change societies.

This lesson focuses on What Are Civil Rights: define civil rights and explain how they differ from general human rights.

## Key ideas

### Nonviolent discipline is a strategy, not passivity

Many civil rights campaigns trained participants to remain nonviolent under provocation. The aim was to reveal the injustice of segregation and win sympathy from the wider public. Discipline also protected the movement legally and kept the focus on the cause rather than on clashes. Understanding this helps explain why marches were carefully planned rather than spontaneous.

### Rights are won, not simply granted

Legal rights often follow long campaigns of organising, protest and persuasion. In the United States, court cases and legislation against segregation came after years of boycotts, marches and voter registration drives. In Britain, the campaign for votes for women combined peaceful petitioning with militant tactics before Parliament extended the franchise. Laws matter, but they usually record victories that movements have already made politically unavoidable.

**Key term — civil rights:** Legal rights guaranteeing equal treatment and participation in public life, such as the right to vote and freedom from discrimination. They are granted and enforced by the state, unlike the broader moral claims of human rights.

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Confusing civil disobedience with rejecting all law** Civil rights campaigners often broke unjust laws openly while appealing to higher legal principles such as constitutional equality. Many accepted arrest to dramatise injustice. This is different from lawlessness; it is a deliberate, public challenge aimed at changing the law.
  - **Thinking rights were handed down by generous rulers** In most cases, excluded groups organised for years before the law changed. The franchise was extended in Britain after sustained suffragette campaigning, and US segregation ended after mass mobilisation, not simply because politicians decided to act.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Distinguish civil rights from human rights." hint="Think about the difference between universal moral claims and rights written into a country's law.">
    Legal entitlements to equal treatment and participation in public life, such as voting and freedom from discrimination. Human rights is the broader category; civil rights are the ones a particular state guarantees to its citizens and residents.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Define apartheid and explain two kinds of pressure that helped end it." hint="Define the system first, then give internal and external pressures.">
    Apartheid was the South African system of legalised racial segregation and white minority rule. It ended through a combination of internal resistance, trade union and township organising, international sanctions and boycotts, and negotiated settlement, leading to the first democratic election in 1994.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Evaluate the claim that legal change alone is enough to secure civil rights." hint="Consider what each track achieves that the other cannot.">
    A movement might win a court ruling but see it ignored without political pressure, or win public sympathy but no legal change without litigation and legislation. Durable victories usually require both: mobilisation makes change politically necessary, and law makes it enforceable.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Identify four factors behind the success of the Montgomery bus boycott of 1955 to 1956." hint="List the economic, organisational, publicity and legal elements.">
    It combined economic pressure on the bus company, disciplined mass organisation through churches and car pools, national publicity, and a parallel court case, Browder v. Gayle, which made bus segregation unlawful.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
