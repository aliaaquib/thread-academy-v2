## Key ideas

### Electoral systems shape results

First-past-the-post elects the candidate with the most votes in each constituency, which usually produces clear winners but can leave many votes unrepresented. Proportional representation allocates seats more closely in line with vote shares, which represents smaller parties better but often requires coalitions. The system chosen affects party numbers, government stability and how well parliament mirrors the electorate.

### Campaigns inform and persuade

Candidates and parties use speeches, debates, posters and media to present their ideas and criticise opponents. Manifestos set out proposed policies, though they are political promises rather than legally binding contracts. Voters should compare claims with evidence, because campaigns can also mislead through selective facts and emotional appeals.

**Key term — proportional representation:** An electoral system that allocates seats in proportion to the votes each party receives. It represents smaller parties more accurately than first-past-the-post but often leads to coalition government.

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Thinking the winner always has majority support** Under first-past-the-post, a candidate can win a constituency with well under half the votes if the rest are split among rivals. A party can even win a parliamentary majority without winning most votes nationally. Majority of seats and majority of votes are different things.
  - **Treating manifestos as legally binding contracts** A manifesto is a statement of intent used to win support, not a law. Parties may change plans because of events, costs or coalition deals. Voters can judge them at the next election, but courts do not enforce manifesto promises.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Evaluate two criticisms of first-past-the-post." hint="Give the criticisms, then the standard defence.">
    It can distort representation because seats may not match votes, discourage support for smaller parties, and produce governments with less than majority popular support. Defenders reply that it usually delivers stable single-party government and a clear local representative.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Outline the key steps in running a trustworthy count." hint="Follow the ballot from box to declaration.">
    Count every valid ballot accurately, apply the agreed voting system, declare the winner transparently, and allow scrutiny such as recounts and observers. Transparency at each stage lets losers trust the process.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Define an election and list three features of a democratic one." hint="Think about choice plus fair process.">
    The process by which citizens choose representatives or decide issues by vote. Democratic elections should be free, fair, regular and competitive, with universal suffrage and honest counting.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Define constituency and ballot." hint="One is about voters grouped together, the other about how they vote.">
    A constituency is the area or group of voters electing a representative. A ballot is the paper or electronic method by which votes are cast and counted in secret.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
