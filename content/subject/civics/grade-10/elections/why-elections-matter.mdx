Elections are how democracies choose their leaders and settle big disagreements peacefully. But campaigns, voting systems and counts all shape the outcome in ways worth understanding. This chapter follows the journey from campaign promises to ballot boxes to results, and compares the systems that turn votes into seats.

This lesson focuses on Why Elections Matter: learn how elections give citizens a voice in choosing leaders and policies.

</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Elections turn votes into authority

Democratic elections give governments their legitimacy by letting citizens choose representatives and, indirectly, policies. For this to work, elections must be free, fair and regular, with genuine choice and honest counting. When these conditions hold, even losers can accept the result because the process was fair.

### Campaigns inform and persuade

Candidates and parties use speeches, debates, posters and media to present their ideas and criticise opponents. Manifestos set out proposed policies, though they are political promises rather than legally binding contracts. Voters should compare claims with evidence, because campaigns can also mislead through selective facts and emotional appeals.

**Key term — franchise:** The right to vote in elections. Universal adult suffrage means nearly all adult citizens may vote, a right won through long campaigns in many countries.

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Believing one vote never matters** Single votes rarely decide national results, but elections are decided by the accumulation of individual choices, and local contests can be very close. Low turnout also hands more influence to those who do vote. Abstention is itself a choice that shapes the outcome.
  - **Treating manifestos as legally binding contracts** A manifesto is a statement of intent used to win support, not a law. Parties may change plans because of events, costs or coalition deals. Voters can judge them at the next election, but courts do not enforce manifesto promises.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="What is a party manifesto, and what is its legal force?" hint="Consider its purpose in a campaign versus its legal status.">
    A manifesto sets out a party's proposed policies to persuade voters. It guides expectations and later judgement of the government, but it is a political promise, not an enforceable legal contract.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Define constituency and ballot." hint="One is about voters grouped together, the other about how they vote.">
    A constituency is the area or group of voters electing a representative. A ballot is the paper or electronic method by which votes are cast and counted in secret.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Compare first-past-the-post with proportional representation." hint="Compare winner-takes-all with share-of-votes logic.">
    FPTP elects whoever gets the most votes in each constituency, which is simple and tends to produce decisive winners, but many votes do not contribute to the result. PR allocates seats in line with vote shares, representing smaller parties better, but often produces coalitions and more complex government formation.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Outline the key steps in running a trustworthy count." hint="Follow the ballot from box to declaration.">
    Count every valid ballot accurately, apply the agreed voting system, declare the winner transparently, and allow scrutiny such as recounts and observers. Transparency at each stage lets losers trust the process.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
