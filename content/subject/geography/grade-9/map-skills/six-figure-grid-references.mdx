<Definition term="Six-Figure Grid References">
  Give precise locations by dividing each grid square into tenths.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Six figures give 100-metre precision

To be more precise, divide the 1 km square into tenths. The extra digits estimate how far across and up the square the feature lies — a six-figure reference pinpoints a 100 m by 100 m square. Estimate the third and sixth digits carefully; they are the ones most people get wrong.

### Along the corridor, then up the stairs

Grid references always give eastings first, then northings. Imagine walking along a corridor (across the map) before climbing stairs (up the map). A four-figure reference like 4738 names the 1 km square whose south-west corner is at easting 47 and northing 38.

**Key term — eastings:** The vertical grid lines on a map, numbered from west to east — read these first in a grid reference.

<WorkedExample title="Finding a six-figure grid reference">
  A church stands inside the 1 km square with south-west corner 47 east, 38 north. Within the square it sits about three-tenths of the way across and seven-tenths of the way up. What is its six-figure grid reference?

  Write the easting of the square's left edge: 47.
  Add the tenths across: 3, giving 473.
  Write the northing of the square's bottom edge: 38.
  Add the tenths up: 7, giving 387.
  Combine eastings then northings: 473387.

  **Answer:** 473387.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Writing a four-figure reference when six are needed** Four figures only name the whole 1 km square. If the question asks for a specific building, you need the extra precision of six figures.
  - **Giving northings before eastings** It must be eastings first — 'along the corridor, then up the stairs'. Reversing them points to a completely different square.
</ImportantNote>
