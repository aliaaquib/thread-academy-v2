Click a link and a page appears — but the page did not teleport. It was chopped up, addressed, thrown across the planet in pieces, and rebuilt on your screen. Here is how.

<Definition term="Data transmission">
  Sending data from one device to another across a network, usually by splitting it into packets.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Packets: data in envelopes

Big files are split into small chunks called **packets**. Each packet carries three things: the data itself, the **sender's address**, the **destination address**, and a **sequence number** (packet 3 of 12). Packets can take different routes and arrive out of order — the sequence numbers let the destination rebuild the original.

Think of posting a jigsaw puzzle one piece per envelope, each numbered: the pieces may arrive jumbled, but the numbers restore the picture.

### IP addresses: every device has one

An **IP address** is a device's numeric address on a network, e.g. 192.168.1.25. Packets are addressed with IPs the way letters use street addresses — without them, routers would not know where to send anything.

### Routers: the postal sorters

**Routers** read each packet's destination IP and forward it toward its target, hop by hop across networks. Your message to another continent may pass through a dozen routers, each making one local decision: "not mine — send it that way."

### Keeping it safe: encryption

Packets cross equipment owned by strangers, so sensitive data is **encrypted** — scrambled with a key so only the intended recipient can read it. That is what the "s" in https does: anyone intercepting your packets sees gibberish, not your password.

**Key term — packet:** A small chunk of data sent across a network, carrying source and destination addresses plus a sequence number for reassembly.

### When data goes wrong: transmission errors

Data does not always arrive as sent. Something is wrong with the data — part of it is incorrect — when accidental errors creep in during transmission:

- **Surges in electricity** — a spike on a wired line can flip bits.
- **Interference in radio waves** — other signals or electrical noise corrupt wireless data.
- **Crosstalk** — signals leaking between adjacent wires, mixing messages together.

These are accidental errors: nobody attacked the data, but the original is changed or damaged anyway. Networks fight back with error detection — extra check data sent alongside the message.

### A real check: counting the 1s

One simple detection method: agree that every chunk of data must contain an even number of 1s. Take the message "MY FAVOURITE SUBJECT IS COMPUTING!" — converted to binary, the sender counts the 1s in each chunk and adds one extra **check bit** to make the total even.

If interference flips a bit in transit, the count becomes odd — and the receiver knows *something is wrong with the data*, even though it cannot tell which bit flipped. The damaged chunk is discarded and re-sent. One flipped bit, one odd count, one caught error: that is error detection working.

<WorkedExample title="Trace an email">
  You send a 3-packet email from Bishkek to a friend in Osh.

  The email is split into packets 1, 2, 3, each stamped with your IP (source) and your friend's IP (destination). Router A forwards them toward Osh; packet 2 takes a faster route and arrives first. Your friend's computer holds packets 1 and 3, waits for 2, then uses the sequence numbers to rebuild the email in order.

  **Answer:** Splitting + addressing + sequencing = reliable delivery even when packets travel separately and arrive shuffled.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **"Data travels whole"** Correction: large data is split into packets — that is why a video can start playing before it has fully arrived.
  - **Packets must arrive in order** Correction: they often do not; sequence numbers exist precisely because order is not guaranteed.
  - **Encryption = the site is honest** Correction: encryption only scrambles data in transit — it says nothing about who runs the destination.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Name the three things a packet carries besides its data.">
    Source address, destination address, and a sequence number.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is an IP address, in one sentence?">
    A numeric address identifying a device on a network, used to route packets to it.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Packets 1–4 of a file arrive in the order 2, 4, 1, 3. Is the file corrupted?">
    No — the receiver uses the sequence numbers to reassemble them as 1, 2, 3, 4.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why encrypt data before transmission?">
    Packets cross strangers' equipment; encryption makes intercepted packets unreadable gibberish.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Name two causes of accidental errors in transmitted data.">
    Any two: surges in electricity, interference in radio waves, crosstalk.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="A chunk of data should have an even number of 1s, but the receiver counts an odd number. What does this mean?">
    A bit was flipped in transit — something is wrong with the data, so the chunk should be discarded and re-sent.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
