A spreadsheet is a grid that calculates. Shops use them for stock, teachers for marks, scientists for measurements — anywhere numbers need organising and the computer should do the arithmetic.

<Definition term="Spreadsheet">
  A grid of rows and columns used to organise, calculate and present data. Each box in the grid is a cell.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### The anatomy of a spreadsheet

- **Columns** run top to bottom and are labelled with letters: A, B, C…
- **Rows** run left to right and are numbered: 1, 2, 3…
- **Cell** — one box, named by its column letter and row number. The cell in column B, row 4 is **B4**. This name is its **cell reference**.
- **Range** — a block of cells, written with a colon: B2:B6 means "cells B2 down to B6".

### Formulas: the spreadsheet calculates

A formula always starts with `=`. It can use numbers, cell references and operators:

<CodeBlock lang="Example">
= B2 + B3
= B2 * 1.2
= (B2 + B3) / 2
</CodeBlock>

The power: if the value in B2 changes, every formula using B2 recalculates instantly. That is why spreadsheets beat calculators for repeated work.

### Functions: ready-made formulas

A **function** is a built-in calculation you call by name:

- `=SUM(B2:B6)` — adds everything in the range
- `=AVERAGE(B2:B6)` — the mean of the range
- `=MIN(B2:B6)` / `=MAX(B2:B6)` — the smallest / largest value

<CodeBlock lang="Example">
Test scores in B2:B6 are 65, 70, 58, 82, 74
=SUM(B2:B6)       gives 349
=AVERAGE(B2:B6)   gives 69.8
=MAX(B2:B6)       gives 82
</CodeBlock>

### A real example: Sofia's bike loan

A spreadsheet tracks a bike bought on credit:

<CodeBlock lang="Example">
Cost of bike ......... 420
Deposit paid ......... 80
Amount borrowed = Cost of bike − Deposit → 340
</CodeBlock>

The formula subtracts the deposit from the cost to give the amount borrowed. Change the deposit to 100 and the amount borrowed recalculates to 320 — the spreadsheet does the "what if" for you.

### Conditional formatting: colours that mean something

**Conditional formatting** colours cells automatically based on their values. The classic rule for a balance column:

- Balance > 0 → green text and background
- Balance < 0 → red text and background
- Balance = 0 → orange text and background

Why? To attract the user's attention to certain parts of the spreadsheet, and to make it easy to see important information — a sea of red balances screams "problem" before you read a single number.

<WorkedExample title="Build the marks sheet">
  Scores for five students sit in B2:B6. In B7 you type =SUM(B2:B6); in B8, =AVERAGE(B2:B6); in B9, =MAX(B2:B6).

  The sheet shows: total 349, average 69.8, best 82. Now a score is corrected from 58 to 68 — you change one cell, and B7, B8 and B9 all update on their own.

  **Answer:** Total 349, average 69.8, maximum 82 — and every formula stays correct when data changes, because formulas reference cells, not typed-in numbers.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Typing numbers instead of cell references** Correction: =65+70+58 breaks when a score changes; =SUM(B2:B6) never does — always reference cells.
  - **Forgetting the = sign** Correction: without =, the spreadsheet treats your formula as plain text and calculates nothing.
  - **Wrong range** Correction: =SUM(B2:B5) silently misses B6 — double-check your ranges include every row.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="What is the cell reference of the cell in column D, row 9?">
    D9 — column letter first, then row number.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Write the range covering cells C1 down to C10.">
    C1:C10 — start cell, colon, end cell.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Prices are in D2:D7. Write a formula for the total.">
    =SUM(D2:D7) — SUM adds every cell in the range.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why is =B2*1.2 better than typing the answer by hand?">
    If B2 changes, the formula recalculates automatically — a typed answer would go stale and wrong.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Sofia's bike costs 420 and she pays an 80 deposit. Write the formula for the amount borrowed.">
    = Cost − Deposit, e.g. =B2−B3 → 340. If the deposit changes, the borrowed amount updates itself.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="A balance column uses conditional formatting: green above 0, red below 0, orange at 0. What is the point?">
    To attract attention to important values and make patterns visible at a glance — red balances signal a problem instantly.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Spreadsheet: a grid of cells (named by column letter + row number) for organising, calculating and presenting data.
  - Formulas start with = and use cell references so they recalculate when data changes.
  - Functions SUM, AVERAGE, MIN and MAX perform ready-made calculations on ranges.
  - Watch out for: typing numbers instead of references, forgetting =, and ranges that miss rows.
</Summary>
