Machines once needed a human hand on every lever. Now factories run overnight with no one inside, and software recognises faces in photos. Two ideas explain this: **automation** and **artificial intelligence**.

<Definition term="Automation">
  Using machines or software to perform tasks with little or no human involvement.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Automation: machines doing the routine

Automation follows fixed rules to do repetitive work:

- **Manufacture** — robot arms weld car bodies and fit parts, faster and more precisely than humans, all night long.
- **Health** — machines dispense medicines, labs analyse blood samples automatically.
- **Advertising** — software chooses which advert to show you based on rules about your browsing.

Why automate? Speed, consistency (machines do not get tired or bored), and safety (robots handle dangerous chemicals). The trade-off: jobs change, and automated systems fail in ways their rules never imagined.

### Artificial intelligence: learning from surroundings

<Definition term="Artificial intelligence">
  Computing systems that take information from their surroundings and produce outputs based on how they process that information — including learning from data.
</Definition>

Classic automation follows rules humans wrote. AI goes further: it **learns patterns from data**. Show it a million labelled photos of cats, and it learns to recognise cats in new photos — nobody wrote "pointy ears = cat".

### Real AI applications

- **Image recognition** — unlocking phones with a face, medical scans flagged for doctors to review.
- **Computer games** — opponents that adapt to how you play instead of following one script.
- **Recommendations** — "you watched this, try that", learned from millions of viewers' habits.
- **Assistants** — speech understood and answered, from timers to translations.

### Think critically

AI is powerful but not magic: it learns from data, so **biased data teaches biased decisions** (a hiring tool trained on old hires can learn old prejudices). It can be confidently wrong. And automation raises real questions: which jobs should machines do, and who is responsible when they err? Knowing what AI *is* lets you judge what it *should* do.

<WorkedExample title="Automation or AI?">
  Three systems: (a) a factory arm that always welds the same spot, (b) a spam filter that learned from millions of flagged emails, (c) a thermostat that turns heating on below 18°C.

  (a) Fixed repeating motion, rules set by engineers → automation. (b) Learned patterns from data, improves with examples → AI. (c) Simple fixed rule (if temperature < 18, heat) → automation, not AI.

  **Answer:** (a) automation, (b) AI, (c) automation. The test: did it learn from data, or just follow rules someone wrote?
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **"AI thinks like a human"** Correction: AI processes information from its surroundings to produce outputs — pattern-matching on data, not understanding.
  - **Calling all automation AI** Correction: a fixed-rule thermostat is automation; AI must learn from data.
  - **"AI is always right"** Correction: AI inherits its training data's flaws — biased data, biased decisions.
</ImportantNote>

<Summary>
  - Automation: machines/software doing routine tasks with little human involvement (manufacture, health, advertising…).
  - AI: systems that take in information from surroundings and learn from data to produce outputs.
  - Real AI: image recognition, adaptive game opponents, recommendations, assistants.
  - Think critically: AI is pattern-matching, not understanding — and it inherits its data's biases.
</Summary>
