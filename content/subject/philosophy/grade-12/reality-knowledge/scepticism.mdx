## Key ideas

### The dream argument and the evil demon

Descartes asked: how do you know you are not dreaming right now? Dreams can feel completely real. He then imagined an evil demon deceiving him about everything — even mathematics. These are not claims that demons exist; they are tests of certainty. Descartes concluded that one thing survives: the thinking self, expressed in 'cogito, ergo sum' — I think, therefore I am.

### Reason vs experience: the founding debate

Descartes, the rationalist, trusted clear reasoning: even if his senses deceived him, 'I think, therefore I am' survived doubt. Locke, the empiricist, replied that the mind starts blank and everything we know comes through the senses. Hume pushed empiricism further, arguing that even cause and effect are just habits of expectation, not things reason can prove.

**Key term — Scepticism:** The challenge that we may not know the things we think we know. Descartes used sceptical arguments as a test: what, if anything, survives doubt?

<WorkedExample title="Can you know you are not dreaming?">
  Apply Descartes's dream argument step by step to the claim 'I know I am awake'.

  State the claim: 'I know I am sitting in a classroom.'.
  Apply the dream test: dreams can feel exactly like waking life, with classrooms, friends, and lessons.
  Ask what distinguishes dreaming from waking: no inner mark reliably tells them apart while the experience lasts.
  Conclude: if you cannot rule out dreaming, your certainty fails — the claim is doubtful.
  Note Descartes's next move: he rebuilds from 'I think, therefore I am', the one claim that survives even the dream test.

  **Answer:** Descartes's argument shows that sensory experience alone cannot guarantee you are awake, since dreams mimic it perfectly. The point is not that you are dreaming, but that certainty needs a stronger foundation — which Descartes finds in the thinking self.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Thinking Descartes believed in an evil demon** The evil demon is a thought experiment, not a belief. Descartes imagines it to test which beliefs survive radical doubt.
  - **Answering Hume by saying 'induction works in practice'** That misses Hume's point: he is asking for the justification of induction, and 'it has worked before' is itself an inductive argument — circular reasoning.
</ImportantNote>
