<Definition term="Rationalism and Empiricism">
  Compare Descartes's trust in reason with Locke and Hume's trust in experience.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Reason vs experience: the founding debate

Descartes, the rationalist, trusted clear reasoning: even if his senses deceived him, 'I think, therefore I am' survived doubt. Locke, the empiricist, replied that the mind starts blank and everything we know comes through the senses. Hume pushed empiricism further, arguing that even cause and effect are just habits of expectation, not things reason can prove.

### Hume's problem of induction

We expect the sun to rise because it always has. But Hume asked: what justifies this expectation? It cannot be reason alone, since a world where the sun fails to rise is not contradictory. And it cannot be experience, since that would assume the very principle — that the future resembles the past — that we are trying to prove. Science relies on induction, yet Hume showed its foundation is habit, not logic.

**Key term — Empiricism:** The view that experience is the main source of knowledge. Locke claimed the mind begins as a blank slate, and Hume argued that all ideas come from impressions of experience.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Rationalism and Empiricism">
  What is the difference between rationalism and empiricism?

  Rationalism holds that reason is the chief source of knowledge; empiricism holds that experience is.

  **Answer:** Rationalism holds that reason is the chief source of knowledge; empiricism holds that experience is.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Answering Hume by saying 'induction works in practice'** That misses Hume's point: he is asking for the justification of induction, and 'it has worked before' is itself an inductive argument — circular reasoning.
  - **Thinking Descartes believed in an evil demon** The evil demon is a thought experiment, not a belief. Descartes imagines it to test which beliefs survive radical doubt.
</ImportantNote>

  <PracticeItem question="Which philosopher argued the mind begins as a blank slate?" hint="His name starts with L.">
    John Locke.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What survives Descartes's radical doubt, and why?" hint="Think about the act of doubting itself.">
    'I think, therefore I am': even doubting proves that a thinking self exists to do the doubting.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why is appealing to past success a circular defence of induction?" hint="What kind of reasoning is 'it worked before'?">
    Because 'it worked before, so it will work again' assumes that the future resembles the past — the very principle under question.
  </PracticeItem>
