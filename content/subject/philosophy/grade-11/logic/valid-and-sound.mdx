## Key ideas

### Valid does not mean true

An argument can be perfectly valid yet have a false conclusion, if a premise is false: 'All cats can fly; Whiskers is a cat; therefore Whiskers can fly' is valid but unsound. Validity tests the structure; soundness also demands true premises.

### Find the conclusion first

Arguments hide their conclusions in the middle of sentences. Look for signpost words — 'so', 'therefore', 'because' — but check carefully: 'because' introduces a premise, not a conclusion. Once you know what is being claimed, you can judge whether the premises really support it.

**Key term — Sound:** Describes a valid argument whose premises are also actually true. A sound argument gives you a conclusion you can trust.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Valid and Sound Arguments">
  What is the difference between a valid argument and a sound argument?

  A valid argument has a structure that guarantees the conclusion if the premises are true; a sound argument is valid and also has true premises.

  **Answer:** A valid argument has a structure that guarantees the conclusion if the premises are true; a sound argument is valid and also has true premises.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Calling a true conclusion 'valid'** Validity describes the argument's structure, not the conclusion's truth. A true conclusion can come from a terrible argument.
  - **Thinking insults win debates** An ad hominem attack may feel satisfying, but it leaves the argument untouched. Address the reasons, not the person.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Is this valid? 'All cats can fly. Whiskers is a cat. Therefore Whiskers can fly.'" hint="Check the structure, not the truth.">
    Yes, it is valid in structure, but unsound because the first premise is false.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="In 'It is raining, so take an umbrella', which part is the premise?" hint="The premise is the reason.">
    The premise is 'it is raining'; the conclusion is 'take an umbrella'.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Name the bad move: 'Don't listen to Jay's idea — he failed last term.'" hint="Does it attack the idea or the person?">
    Ad hominem: it attacks Jay instead of engaging with his idea.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Name the bad move: 'You want a later bedtime? You might as well never sleep!'" hint="Has her view been fairly stated?">
    Straw man: it distorts her view into an extreme version that is easy to knock down.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
