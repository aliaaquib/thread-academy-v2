This lesson focuses on Spotting Weak Evidence: judge whether the evidence behind a claim is strong enough to trust.

<Definition term="Spotting Weak Evidence">
  Judge whether the evidence behind a claim is strong enough to trust.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Find the conclusion first

Arguments hide their conclusions in the middle of sentences. Look for signpost words — 'so', 'therefore', 'because' — but check carefully: 'because' introduces a premise, not a conclusion. Once you know what is being claimed, you can judge whether the premises really support it.

### Attack the argument, not the person

An ad hominem move attacks the speaker instead of their reasoning: 'You only say that because you are unpopular.' A straw man distorts the other view: 'You want later bedtimes? So you want no sleep at all!' Both dodge the real argument, and spotting them keeps debates honest.

**Key term — Conclusion:** The claim an argument is trying to prove, often signalled by words like 'so', 'therefore', or 'hence'.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Spotting Weak Evidence">
  Imagine an advert says 'Nine out of ten dentists agree'. Why might this be weak evidence?

  We don't know how many dentists were asked, how the question was worded, or who paid for the survey — the evidence is too vague to trust.

  **Answer:** We don't know how many dentists were asked, how the question was worded, or who paid for the survey — the evidence is too vague to trust.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Accepting any evidence at face value** One example is not a trend, and a confident tone is not proof. Ask: how much evidence is there, and where did it come from?
  - **Calling a true conclusion 'valid'** Validity describes the argument's structure, not the conclusion's truth. A true conclusion can come from a terrible argument.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="In 'It is raining, so take an umbrella', which part is the premise?" hint="The premise is the reason.">
    The premise is 'it is raining'; the conclusion is 'take an umbrella'.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is the difference between a valid argument and a sound argument?" hint="One needs true premises as well.">
    A valid argument has a structure that guarantees the conclusion if the premises are true; a sound argument is valid and also has true premises.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Is this valid? 'All cats can fly. Whiskers is a cat. Therefore Whiskers can fly.'" hint="Check the structure, not the truth.">
    Yes, it is valid in structure, but unsound because the first premise is false.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Name the bad move: 'Don't listen to Jay's idea — he failed last term.'" hint="Does it attack the idea or the person?">
    Ad hominem: it attacks Jay instead of engaging with his idea.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
