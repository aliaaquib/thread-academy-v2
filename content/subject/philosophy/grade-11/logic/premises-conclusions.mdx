Every day you meet arguments: adverts, influencers, friends trying to persuade you. Logic is the toolkit for sorting good arguments from bad ones. By the end of this chapter you will be able to find conclusions, test validity, and spot the tricks people use when their evidence is weak.

This lesson focuses on Premises and Conclusions: learn to split any argument into the reasons given and the claim they support.

<Definition term="Premises and Conclusions">
  Learn to split any argument into the reasons given and the claim they support.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Find the conclusion first

Arguments hide their conclusions in the middle of sentences. Look for signpost words — 'so', 'therefore', 'because' — but check carefully: 'because' introduces a premise, not a conclusion. Once you know what is being claimed, you can judge whether the premises really support it.

### Valid does not mean true

An argument can be perfectly valid yet have a false conclusion, if a premise is false: 'All cats can fly; Whiskers is a cat; therefore Whiskers can fly' is valid but unsound. Validity tests the structure; soundness also demands true premises.

**Key term — Conclusion:** The claim an argument is trying to prove, often signalled by words like 'so', 'therefore', or 'hence'.

<WorkedExample title="Testing an argument about homework">
  Mia argues: 'Homework should be banned. It takes up evenings, it causes stress, and Finland does well with less of it.' Analyse her argument.

  Find the conclusion: 'Homework should be banned.'.
  List the premises: it takes evenings; it causes stress; Finland does well with less.
  Check each premise: the first two are plausible; the Finland claim needs evidence — is it accurate and comparable?
  Check the structure: even if all premises are true, do they prove a total ban? Less homework might follow, but 'banned' is a bigger claim.
  Give a verdict: the argument is reasonable but not watertight — it supports reducing homework more strongly than banning it.

  **Answer:** Mia's premises support her conclusion only partly: they argue well for less homework, but 'banned' needs stronger evidence. A good reply would ask for the Finland evidence and suggest a smaller conclusion.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Thinking insults win debates** An ad hominem attack may feel satisfying, but it leaves the argument untouched. Address the reasons, not the person.
  - **Calling a true conclusion 'valid'** Validity describes the argument's structure, not the conclusion's truth. A true conclusion can come from a terrible argument.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="What is the difference between a valid argument and a sound argument?" hint="One needs true premises as well.">
    A valid argument has a structure that guarantees the conclusion if the premises are true; a sound argument is valid and also has true premises.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="In 'It is raining, so take an umbrella', which part is the premise?" hint="The premise is the reason.">
    The premise is 'it is raining'; the conclusion is 'take an umbrella'.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Is this valid? 'All cats can fly. Whiskers is a cat. Therefore Whiskers can fly.'" hint="Check the structure, not the truth.">
    Yes, it is valid in structure, but unsound because the first premise is false.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Name the bad move: 'Don't listen to Jay's idea — he failed last term.'" hint="Does it attack the idea or the person?">
    Ad hominem: it attacks Jay instead of engaging with his idea.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
