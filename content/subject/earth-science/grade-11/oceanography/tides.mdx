## Key ideas

### Tides are a gravitational tug-of-war

The Moon's gravity pulls ocean water towards it, creating a bulge on the near side, while inertia flings a second bulge out on the far side. As Earth rotates through both bulges, most coasts experience two high tides and two low tides each day. When the Sun lines up with the Moon, the pulls add together to give extra-large spring tides; when they are at right angles, smaller neap tides result.

### Wind and spin organise surface currents

Prevailing winds drag surface water into huge rotating gyres, and the Coriolis effect bends them into circular paths: clockwise in the northern hemisphere, anticlockwise in the southern. Western boundary currents such as the Gulf Stream are narrow, fast and warm, carrying tropical heat towards the poles and softening the climates of coastlines downstream.

**Key term — Spring tides:** Spring tides are the largest tides of the month, occurring when the Sun, Moon and Earth line up so their gravitational pulls combine.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Understanding Tides">
  Why do most coasts get two high tides a day?

  There are two tidal bulges, one facing the Moon and one on the opposite side, and Earth's rotation carries each coast through both bulges daily.

  **Answer:** There are two tidal bulges, one facing the Moon and one on the opposite side, and Earth's rotation carries each coast through both bulges daily.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Thinking tides are caused by the Moon's phases** Tides are caused by gravity, not by how the Moon looks. The phase only matters because a full or new Moon means the Sun and Moon are lined up, strengthening the pull.
  - **Assuming El Niño only affects the Pacific** El Niño begins in the tropical Pacific, but by shifting atmospheric circulation it alters rainfall and storms across the globe, from drought in Australia to floods in South America.
</ImportantNote>

  <PracticeItem question="What are spring tides and when do they occur?" hint="Think about line-up.">
    The largest tides of the month, occurring when the Sun, Moon and Earth line up so their gravitational pulls combine.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="In which direction do ocean gyres rotate in the northern hemisphere, and why?" hint="Think about the Coriolis effect.">
    Clockwise, because the Coriolis effect deflects moving water to the right in the northern hemisphere.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What drives thermohaline circulation?" hint="Think about density.">
    Differences in water density caused by temperature and salinity: cold, salty water sinks and drives the slow deep circulation.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Explain why upwelling zones support rich fisheries." hint="Think about nutrients.">
    Upwelling brings cold, nutrient-rich deep water to the sunlit surface, feeding plankton growth that supports the whole food chain.
  </PracticeItem>
