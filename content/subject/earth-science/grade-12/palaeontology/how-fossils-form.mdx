Fossils are the pages of life's diary, recording billions of years of evolution in stone. In this chapter you will learn how the unlikely accident of fossilisation happens, how index fossils date the rocks, why mass extinctions keep resetting life's story, and what the bones say about our own origins.

This lesson focuses on How Fossils Form: learn the rare conditions that preserve a living thing as a fossil.

## Key ideas

### Fossilisation is a rare accident

Fossilisation requires rapid burial in sediment, which protects remains from scavengers and decay, followed by mineral-rich water seeping through and replacing the original material. Because these conditions are uncommon, the fossil record is biased towards hard parts like bones and shells and towards creatures that lived in seas and lakes where burial was likely.

### Index fossils correlate distant rocks

If the same index fossil, such as a particular trilobite species, appears in rock layers on different continents, those layers must be the same age, because the species lived everywhere only briefly. This principle of faunal succession lets geologists match up strata worldwide and build the geological time scale without any radiometric dates.

**Key term — Fossil:** A fossil is the preserved remains or traces of an ancient organism, such as bones, shells, footprints or burrows, usually found in sedimentary rock.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: How Fossils Form">
  Why are most fossils found in sedimentary rather than igneous rocks?

  Sedimentary rocks form by gentle burial and layering that preserves remains; igneous rocks form from molten material that destroys them.

  **Answer:** Sedimentary rocks form by gentle burial and layering that preserves remains; igneous rocks form from molten material that destroys them.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Thinking every dead animal becomes a fossil** Fossilisation needs rapid burial and mineral replacement, which is rare. The vast majority of organisms decay completely and leave no trace.
  - **Assuming the fossil record is complete** Fossilisation favours hard parts and watery environments, so soft-bodied creatures and land dwellers are under-represented. Gaps in the record are expected, not proof against evolution.
</ImportantNote>

  <PracticeItem question="Where does fossil evidence say our species originated, and what is one key piece of evidence?" hint="Think Africa and upright walking.">

  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What two qualities make a fossil a good index fossil?" hint="Think widespread and brief.">
    It must have been geographically widespread but existed for only a short geological time.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="How did the end-Cretaceous extinction change the course of evolution?" hint="Think about who survived.">
    It wiped out the non-avian dinosaurs, freeing ecological niches that mammals then diversified to fill.
