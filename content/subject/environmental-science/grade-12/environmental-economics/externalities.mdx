Why do factories pollute rivers when clean water benefits everyone? Because the costs fall on others while the profits stay private. Environmental economics studies these market failures and designs policies to fix them. In this chapter you will learn how economists value nature and how tools like green taxes make polluters pay.

This lesson focuses on Understanding Externalities: learn how pollution escapes the price tag.

## Key ideas

### Pollution is a market failure

Markets work well when buyers and sellers bear all the costs, but pollution dumps costs on third parties. Because the polluter does not pay for the damage, too much pollution is produced. Economists call this a negative externality, and it justifies government intervention.

### Nature has value even without a price tag

Economists value ecosystem services through methods such as asking what people would pay to protect them, or calculating the cost of replacing them artificially. A mangrove forest, for example, can be valued by the storm damage it prevents. Such valuations help decision-makers compare conservation with development.

**Key term — Externality:** A cost or benefit of an economic activity that falls on people not involved in it, such as smoke from a factory harming nearby residents. Negative externalities are a classic market failure.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Understanding Externalities">
  How does the polluter-pays principle work in practice?

  Those who cause pollution bear its costs through taxes, clean-up bills or permit purchases, rather than the public paying. This gives polluters a financial incentive to reduce damage.

  **Answer:** Those who cause pollution bear its costs through taxes, clean-up bills or permit purchases, rather than the public paying. This gives polluters a financial incentive to reduce damage.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Confusing a carbon tax with cap-and-trade** A tax fixes the price of emissions; cap-and-trade fixes the total quantity. Both price pollution but work through different mechanisms.
  - **Thinking a zero price means zero value** Clean air has no market price but enormous value. Ignoring unpriced ecosystem services is exactly what causes environmental damage.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Define a negative externality and give one example." hint="Think of a cost dumped on people who did not agree to it.">
    A cost of an economic activity that falls on uninvolved third parties. Example: a factory's smoke damaging the health of nearby residents.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Explain the tragedy of the commons using a fishery." hint="Think about what each fisher gains versus what all fishers lose.">
    Each fisher gains the full profit from catching more fish, while the cost of depletion is shared by all. Every fisher therefore overfishes, and the shared stock collapses.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why is clean air described as a public good?" hint="Can anyone be stopped from breathing it, and does your breathing use it up?">
    It is non-excludable because nobody can be prevented from breathing it, and non-rival because one person's breathing does not meaningfully reduce another's. Markets therefore underprovide clean air.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Name two methods economists use to value ecosystem services." hint="One asks people, the other prices a replacement.">
    Stated preference surveys ask what people would pay to protect nature; replacement cost methods calculate what it would cost to provide the service artificially, such as building flood walls instead of keeping wetlands.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
