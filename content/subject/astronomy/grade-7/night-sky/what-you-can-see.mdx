On a clear, dark night the sky is full of points of light, and each one has a story to tell. Some are stars like our Sun, some are planets reflecting sunlight, and the brightest is often the Moon. Learning to read the night sky is the first step in becoming an astronomer.

This lesson focuses on What You Can See: the stars, Moon and bright objects visible on a clear night.

<Definition term="What You Can See">
  The stars, Moon and bright objects visible on a clear night.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Why stars seem to move

Stars appear to move across the sky during the night, but it is actually Earth that is turning. Earth spins once every 24 hours, so the whole sky appears to rotate around a point near the pole star. This is the same reason the Sun rises and sets.

### The Moon changes shape

The Moon does not make its own light; we see it because sunlight bounces off it. As the Moon orbits Earth, we see different amounts of its sunlit half, from a thin crescent to a full disc. The full cycle of phases takes just under a month.

**Key term — Light pollution:** The brightening of the night sky by street lights, buildings and cars. It washes out faint stars, so the best views of the night sky are found far from towns.

<WorkedExample title="Finding north with the Plough">
  It is a clear night and you can see the Plough, the seven bright stars shaped like a saucepan. How can you find the North Star, Polaris?

  First find the two stars that form the front edge of the saucepan's bowl, farthest from the handle. Astronomers call these the pointer stars.
  Imagine a line running through these two stars, going from the bottom pointer to the top pointer.
  Follow that imaginary line about five times the distance between the two pointers.
  The fairly bright star you land on is Polaris, the North Star. It sits almost directly above the North Pole, so it stays in the same place all night.
  Face Polaris and you are facing north.

  **Answer:** Follow the line of the Plough's two pointer stars for about five pointer-lengths to reach Polaris, which marks north.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Expecting to see the night sky clearly straight away** Your eyes need about 20 minutes in the dark to adjust. Looking at a phone screen resets the process, so put devices away while stargazing.
  - **Saying the Moon changes shape because of Earth's shadow** The phases of the Moon are caused by the viewing angle as the Moon orbits Earth, not by Earth's shadow. Earth's shadow only falls on the Moon during a rare lunar eclipse.
</ImportantNote>

  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="How long does it take for the Moon to go through all its phases once?" hint="Slightly less than one calendar month.">

  </PracticeItem>
