## Key ideas

### Orbits are endless falling

A satellite stays in orbit because it is falling towards Earth while moving sideways so fast that Earth's surface curves away beneath it. It keeps missing the ground forever. Astronauts float because they and their spacecraft are falling together, not because gravity has vanished.

### Gravity weakens with distance

Gravity follows an inverse-square law: double the distance and the force drops to a quarter. This is why Mercury, closest to the Sun, feels the strongest pull and races around fastest, while distant Neptune creeps along.

**Key term — Orbit:** The curved path of one object around another under gravity. An orbiting object is constantly falling towards the body it circles, but moving sideways fast enough to keep missing it.

<WorkedExample title="Why does the Moon not fall to Earth?">
  Gravity pulls the Moon towards Earth all the time, yet the Moon has orbited for billions of years without crashing. Explain why, using the idea of falling.

  The Moon is indeed falling towards Earth, pulled by Earth's gravity.
  But the Moon is also moving sideways at about 1 km per second.
  As it falls, Earth's surface curves away beneath it at just the right rate.
  The result is a stable loop: the Moon falls around Earth instead of into it.
  This balance of forward motion and gravitational pull is what an orbit is.

  **Answer:** The Moon is constantly falling towards Earth, but its sideways speed carries it around the planet so it keeps missing, forming a stable orbit.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Believing the Sun's gravity only affects nearby planets** The Sun's gravity holds the entire solar system, out to the distant Kuiper belt and beyond; it simply weakens with distance.
  - **Saying there is no gravity in space** There is gravity everywhere in space; astronauts float because they are in free fall around Earth, not because gravity is absent.
</ImportantNote>

    The satellite falls towards Earth under gravity but moves sideways so fast that the curved Earth drops away beneath it, so it falls around the planet instead of into it.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why does Mercury orbit the Sun faster than Neptune?" hint="Link gravity's strength to distance and speed.">
    Mercury is much closer to the Sun, so the Sun's gravity pulls it more strongly, and it must move faster to stay in orbit.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is escape velocity, and what is its value for Earth?" hint="It is the speed needed to leave and never return.">
    The minimum speed to escape a planet's gravity completely; for Earth it is about 11.2 km per second.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What two things does the strength of gravity between two objects depend on?" hint="Think about mass and separation.">
    The masses of the two objects and the distance between them; more mass means stronger gravity, greater distance means weaker.
  </PracticeItem>
