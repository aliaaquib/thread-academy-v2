<Definition term="Life Cycle of Stars">

</Definition>

## Key ideas

### A star's mass decides its fate

Low-mass stars like the Sun end as white dwarfs. Stars more than about 8 times the Sun's mass die violently as supernovae, leaving neutron stars or black holes. Massive stars burn their fuel fast and live only millions of years, while small red dwarfs can shine for trillions.

### The Milky Way is one of billions

Our galaxy is a spiral of roughly 100 to 400 billion stars, about 100,000 light years across. The observable universe contains an estimated two trillion galaxies. When you look at the Andromeda galaxy, its light has travelled 2.5 million years to reach you.

**Key term — White dwarf:** The hot, dense remnant left behind when a low-mass star like the Sun sheds its outer layers. A white dwarf slowly cools over billions of years.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example: Life Cycle of Stars">
  Why do massive stars live shorter lives than small stars, even though they have more fuel?

  Massive stars burn their fuel enormously faster because of the extreme pressure and temperature in their cores, so they exhaust it in millions rather than billions of years.

  **Answer:** Massive stars burn their fuel enormously faster because of the extreme pressure and temperature in their cores, so they exhaust it in millions rather than billions of years.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Thinking all stars die as black holes** Only stars more than about 20 times the Sun's mass can become black holes. Sun-like stars end as white dwarfs.
  - **Saying the Sun will explode as a supernova** The Sun is far too small for a supernova; it will swell into a red giant and shed its layers gently instead.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="What determines whether a star ends as a white dwarf, neutron star or black hole?" hint="It is about how much material the star has.">
    Its mass: low-mass stars become white dwarfs, stars above about 8 solar masses explode as supernovae leaving neutron stars or black holes.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Explain the phrase 'we are made of stardust'." hint="Where do elements heavier than helium come from?">
    Elements heavier than hydrogen and helium were forged inside stars and scattered by supernovae, so the atoms in our bodies were once inside stars.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="How far away is the Andromeda galaxy in light years, and what does that mean?" hint="Its light has been travelling a very long time.">
    About 2.5 million light years away, meaning we see it as it was 2.5 million years ago.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What powers a star like the Sun?" hint="It happens in the core and turns hydrogen into helium.">
    Nuclear fusion: hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium, releasing energy.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
