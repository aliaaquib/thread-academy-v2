## Key ideas

### The habitable zone is a starting point

The habitable zone is the distance range where a planet could have liquid water, depending on the star's brightness. But water alone is not enough: the planet needs a suitable atmosphere and protection from radiation. Astronomers now analyse exoplanet atmospheres with telescopes like JWST, looking for water vapour and potential biosignatures.

### Strange new worlds

Exoplanets include hot Jupiters, gas giants orbiting scorchingly close to their stars, and super-Earths, rocky planets larger than Earth with no solar-system equivalent. The TRAPPIST-1 system has seven Earth-sized rocky planets, several in the habitable zone, orbiting a cool red dwarf 40 light years away.

**Key term — Exoplanet:** A planet orbiting a star other than the Sun. The first confirmed exoplanets around a Sun-like star were found in 1995; thousands are now known.

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Assuming the habitable zone guarantees life** The habitable zone only means liquid water is possible; a planet there also needs the right atmosphere, magnetic field and chemistry for life.
  - **Saying the first exoplanets were found centuries ago** The first confirmed exoplanets around a normal star were discovered in 1995; the field is only a few decades old.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Describe the transit method of detecting exoplanets." hint="Think about a mini-eclipse.">
    Astronomers watch a star's brightness and look for a small, regular dip caused by a planet passing in front of the star, blocking some of its light.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What does the radial velocity method measure, and what does it reveal?" hint="It involves the star wobbling.">
    It measures the Doppler shift of the star's light as the planet's gravity makes the star wobble back and forth, revealing the planet's mass.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is a hot Jupiter?" hint="Think about size and orbit.">
    A gas giant planet orbiting very close to its star, making it far hotter than Jupiter, which orbits far from the Sun.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why would finding oxygen and methane together in an exoplanet atmosphere be exciting?" hint="These two gases react with each other.">
    They destroy each other chemically, so both persisting would need constant replenishment, possibly by living organisms: a potential biosignature.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
