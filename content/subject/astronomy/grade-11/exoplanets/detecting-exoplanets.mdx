Every star you see could have planets, and since the first confirmed discoveries in the 1990s astronomers have found more than 5,000 worlds orbiting other stars. Some are familiar, many are bizarre, and a few sit in the habitable zone where life might exist. Exoplanet science is one of the fastest-moving fields in astronomy.

This lesson focuses on Detecting Exoplanets: the transit and radial velocity methods used to find planets around other stars.

<Definition term="Detecting Exoplanets">
  The transit and radial velocity methods used to find planets around other stars.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### Two main detection tricks

The transit method watches for a star dimming as a planet crosses it; the Kepler space telescope found thousands this way. The radial velocity method measures the star's back-and-forth wobble as the planet's gravity tugs it. Used together, they give both size and mass, hence density, telling us whether a planet is rocky or gaseous.

### Strange new worlds

Exoplanets include hot Jupiters, gas giants orbiting scorchingly close to their stars, and super-Earths, rocky planets larger than Earth with no solar-system equivalent. The TRAPPIST-1 system has seven Earth-sized rocky planets, several in the habitable zone, orbiting a cool red dwarf 40 light years away.

**Key term — Radial velocity method:** Detecting an exoplanet by the slight wobble it induces in its star, measured via the Doppler shift of the star's light. It reveals the planet's mass.

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Thinking we have photographed most exoplanets** Almost all exoplanets are detected indirectly through transits or wobbles; only a few dozen young, distant giants have been directly imaged.
  - **Saying the first exoplanets were found centuries ago** The first confirmed exoplanets around a normal star were discovered in 1995; the field is only a few decades old.
</ImportantNote>

  <PracticeItem question="What does the radial velocity method measure, and what does it reveal?" hint="It involves the star wobbling.">
    It measures the Doppler shift of the star's light as the planet's gravity makes the star wobble back and forth, revealing the planet's mass.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why would finding oxygen and methane together in an exoplanet atmosphere be exciting?" hint="These two gases react with each other.">
    They destroy each other chemically, so both persisting would need constant replenishment, possibly by living organisms: a potential biosignature.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="A transit dip is 0.04 (4 percent). The star is Sun-sized (696,000 km radius). Estimate the planet's radius." hint="Take the square root of 0.04 first.">
    Rp ÷ Rs = √0.04 = 0.2, so Rp = 0.2 × 696,000 ≈ 139,000 km, about twice Jupiter's radius.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is a hot Jupiter?" hint="Think about size and orbit.">
    A gas giant planet orbiting very close to its star, making it far hotter than Jupiter, which orbits far from the Sun.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
