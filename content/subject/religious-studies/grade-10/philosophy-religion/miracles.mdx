## Key ideas

### Hume's argument against miracles

David Hume defined a miracle as a violation of the laws of nature and argued in his Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding that no testimony is ever strong enough to establish one. Laws of nature rest on uniform human experience - countless observations that the dead stay dead - while testimony for miracles is rare, often comes from unreliable witnesses and is contradicted by other religions' miracle claims. A wise person, Hume says, proportions belief to the evidence, so belief in miracles is never rational. Critics reply that this begs the question: if God exists, miracles are not as improbable as Hume assumes.

### Religious experience as evidence

Millions report visions, voices, conversions and mystical union with God. William James studied such experiences sympathetically in The Varieties of Religious Experience, while Richard Swinburne argued we should normally trust experiences (the principle of credulity) and trust others' reports (the principle of testimony) unless we have reason to doubt them. Critics respond that experiences are private and unverifiable, that drugs and brain states can produce similar effects, and that conflicting experiences across religions cannot all be true.

**Key term — empiricism:** The view that all knowledge comes from sense experience; David Hume is its most famous champion in the philosophy of religion.

<WorkedExample title="Evaluating Hume on miracles">
  Hume argues it is never rational to believe a miracle has occurred. Reconstruct his argument in steps and give the strongest objection.

  Step 1: State Hume's definition - a miracle is a violation of the laws of nature by a particular volition of the Deity.
  Step 2: State his evidence principle - a wise person proportions belief to the evidence.
  Step 3: Weigh the evidence against miracles - laws of nature are supported by uniform, exceptionless experience.
  Step 4: Weigh the evidence for miracles - human testimony, which is often unreliable, driven by wonder and contradicted by rival religions' claims.
  Step 5: Draw Hume's conclusion - the uniform experience always outweighs the testimony, so belief is never rational.
  Step 6: Give the strongest objection - Hume assumes miracles are maximally improbable, but if God exists, the prior probability of miracles is higher; the argument only works if God's existence is already ruled out.

  **Answer:** Hume's argument: miracles violate laws of nature established by uniform experience, and testimony can never outweigh that uniform experience, so rational belief in miracles is impossible. Strongest objection: the argument begs the question against the theist - if a God who acts in the world exists, miracles are not vanishingly improbable, so Hume's weighing of the evidence is loaded from the start.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
  - **Hume proved that miracles never happen** Hume argued that belief in miracles can never be rational given the evidence; he did not claim to prove that no miracle has ever occurred.
  - **William James proved God exists through religious experience** James studied religious experiences as a psychologist and found them life-changing for those who have them, but he did not claim they prove God's existence to everyone.
</ImportantNote>

  <PracticeItem question="How does Hume define a miracle?" hint="Think of laws of nature.">
    A violation of the laws of nature by a particular volition of the Deity.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is Swinburne's principle of credulity?" hint="Trust what?">
    That we should trust our experiences as veridical - take them at face value - unless we have positive reason to doubt them.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Name one criticism of religious experience as evidence for God." hint="Think about verifiability.">
    Any of: experiences are private and cannot be publicly checked; similar experiences can be produced by drugs or brain stimulation; conflicting experiences across religions cannot all be true.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is fideism?" hint="Faith versus reason.">
    The view that religious belief rests on faith and does not need - or cannot receive - rational justification.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>
