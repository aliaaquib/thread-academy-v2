## International Relations

Inside a country, the state keeps order. Between countries there’s no higher authority — just 190+ sovereign states. That condition has a name: anarchy.

<Definition term="Anarchy (IR)">
  In international relations, **anarchy** means the absence of a supreme global authority — not chaos. Realists say it drives states to seek power and security; liberals say institutions and trade can tame it.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Realism: states seek security/power; expect rivalry, alliances, arms races.
- Liberalism: democracy, trade and institutions (UN, EU) make cooperation rational.
- Constructivism: ideas and identities shape interests — enemies can become friends.
- All three illuminate: none explains everything alone.

<Example title="The EU experiment">
  France and Germany fought three wars in 70 years — then bound their coal and steel industries together. Economic interdependence made war materially harder: liberalism in action.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="How would a realist and a liberal explain the UN differently?" hint="Power vs cooperation.">
    Realist: the UN reflects great-power interests and can’t override them. Liberal: it lowers the costs of cooperation and builds norms that restrain conflict.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What does ‘anarchy’ mean in IR?" hint="No world government.">
    The absence of a supreme authority above states — states must ultimately rely on themselves.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - IR starts from anarchy: no world government.
  - Realism stresses power and security; liberalism stresses institutions and trade.
  - Constructivism adds ideas and identity.
  - Use theories as lenses, not religions.
</Summary>
