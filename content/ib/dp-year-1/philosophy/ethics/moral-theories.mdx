## Moral Theories

A runaway trolley is heading for five people; you can divert it to kill one instead. What should you do? Your answer reveals your moral theory.

<Definition term="Utilitarianism">
  **Utilitarianism** (Bentham, Mill): an action is right if it maximises overall happiness/wellbeing — the greatest good for the greatest number. Consequences are what count.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Utilitarianism: judge by consequences — maximise wellbeing.
- Kantianism: act only on maxims you could will as universal law; never treat people as mere means.
- Virtue ethics (Aristotle): be a flourishing, virtuous person — courage, honesty, generosity — and right action follows.
- Each handles the trolley differently: the utilitarian pulls the lever; Kant hesitates; the virtue ethicist asks what a good person would do.

<Example title="The transplant dilemma">
  A surgeon could kill one healthy patient to save five with their organs. Utilitarian math says yes — which is exactly why critics say utilitarianism needs constraints like rights.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="How would Kant and a utilitarian differ on lying to a murderer at your door?" hint="Duty vs consequences.">
    Kant: lying violates a universal duty — never lie. Utilitarian: lie, since it prevents far greater harm.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What does virtue ethics focus on instead of rules or consequences?" hint="Character.">
    The character of the moral agent — cultivating virtues like honesty and courage that lead a flourishing life.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Utilitarianism: maximise overall wellbeing.
  - Kantianism: follow universalisable duties; respect persons.
  - Virtue ethics: cultivate good character.
  - No theory wins every case — that’s why ethics is hard.
</Summary>
