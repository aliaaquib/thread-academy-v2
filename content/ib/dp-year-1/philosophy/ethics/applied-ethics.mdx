## Applied Ethics

Ethics isn’t just trolley problems. It’s in your shopping, your phone, and the code running the world.

<Definition term="Moral patient">
  A **moral patient** is a being whose interests matter morally — deserving of consideration, whether or not it’s a moral agent (one who can act morally). The big question: which beings qualify?
</Definition>

## Key points

- Animal ethics (Singer): sentience — the capacity to suffer — grounds moral consideration.
- AI ethics: bias in hiring algorithms, accountability for autonomous weapons, deepfakes and truth.
- Environmental ethics: do future people — or nature itself — have moral standing?
- Applied ethics rarely settles things; it clarifies what’s at stake.

<Example title="The biased hiring algorithm">
  An AI trained on past hiring data ‘learns’ to prefer men — automating historical discrimination at scale. Who’s responsible: the data, the developers, the company deploying it?
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="What is Singer’s argument for animal consideration?" hint="Suffering, not intelligence.">
    If a being can suffer, its suffering counts morally — intelligence is as irrelevant as race or sex in deciding whose pain matters.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Give one ethical problem with autonomous weapons." hint="Who decides to kill?">
    Delegating kill decisions to machines removes human moral agency and accountability — no one to answer for mistakes.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Ethics applies to animals, AI and the environment.
  - Sentience is a common ground for moral standing.
  - AI raises bias, accountability and truth problems.
  - Clarifying the stakes is ethics’ real job.
</Summary>
