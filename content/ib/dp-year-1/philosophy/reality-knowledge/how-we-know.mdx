## How We Know

Every claim to knowledge rests on something: reason, experience, or trust. Philosophy asks whether the foundations hold.

<Definition term="Problem of induction">
  The **problem of induction** (Hume): no number of past observations logically guarantees the future will resemble the past. The sun has risen every day — but that doesn’t PROVE it will tomorrow.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Rationalism (Descartes): reason is the foundation — ‘I think, therefore I am’.
- Empiricism (Locke, Hume): experience is the foundation — the mind starts as a blank slate.
- Scepticism: perhaps we know far less than we think (brains in vats, dreams).
- Science works brilliantly without solving induction — a puzzle, not a paralysis.

<Example title="Descartes’ demon">
  What if an evil demon deceives all your senses? You can doubt everything — except that you’re doubting. ‘I think, therefore I am’: the one certainty.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="State Hume’s problem of induction." hint="Past ≠ proof of future.">
    Past regularities don’t logically entail future ones; induction assumes nature’s uniformity without proving it.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="How do rationalists and empiricists differ on the source of knowledge?" hint="Reason vs senses.">
    Rationalists: reason and innate ideas are primary. Empiricists: sensory experience is primary; the mind begins blank.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Epistemology asks what knowledge is and how we get it.
  - Rationalism trusts reason; empiricism trusts experience.
  - Induction works in practice but resists proof.
  - Scepticism reminds us certainty is rare.
</Summary>
