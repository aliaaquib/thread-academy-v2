## Fallacies

Advertisers, politicians and comment sections run on fallacies. Naming them is intellectual self-defence.

<Definition term="Fallacy">
  A **fallacy** is a common pattern of faulty reasoning — an argument that looks persuasive but fails logically. Spotting the pattern lets you reject the argument without being fooled by its packaging.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Ad hominem: attacking the person, not the argument.
- Straw man: refuting a distorted version of the opponent’s view.
- False dilemma: pretending only two options exist.
- Appeal to popularity/nature/authority: truth isn’t a vote, and nature isn’t moral.

<Example title="The straw man in debate">
  ‘My opponent wants to cut military spending’ becomes ‘my opponent wants to leave us defenceless’. The second claim is easier to attack — and nobody actually made it.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Identify the fallacy: ‘You can’t trust his climate research — he failed maths at school.’" hint="Attacking the person.">
    Ad hominem: his school grades don’t touch the research’s evidence.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why is ‘it’s natural, so it’s good’ fallacious?" hint="Nature isn’t a moral guide.">
    Appeal to nature: natural things include disease and arsenic; moral conclusions need moral premises, not just ‘natural’.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Fallacies are recurring patterns of bad reasoning.
  - Attack arguments, not people (ad hominem).
  - Argue against the real position, not a straw man.
  - Spotting fallacies is thinking self-defence.
</Summary>
