## Migration Debates

Migration debates run hot because they touch jobs, identity and security at once. Heat isn’t light — so let’s look at evidence.

<Definition term="Integration">
  **Integration** is the process by which migrants become full participants in their new society — economically, socially and civically — while debates continue over how much cultural adaptation should be expected.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Economics: most studies find small overall wage effects; migrants are net fiscal contributors in many countries and fill labour shortages.
- Culture: concerns about identity and cohesion are real and legitimate to debate.
- Security: vetting exists because risks exist — the question is proportionality.
- Integration works best with language training, work rights and anti-discrimination enforcement.

<Example title="The Mariel boatlift">
  When 125,000 Cubans arrived in Miami (1980), economist David Card found wages for low-skilled locals barely moved — the labour market absorbed them. Evidence vs intuition.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="What did the Mariel boatlift study find about wages?" hint="Card, 1990.">
    Little to no negative effect on native low-skilled wages in Miami despite a large sudden influx.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Name two policies that aid integration." hint="Language and work.">
    Language classes and early work rights — plus anti-discrimination enforcement.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Migration debates mix economics, culture and security.
  - Evidence on wages is milder than rhetoric suggests.
  - Integration is a two-way process needing policy.
  - Distinguish evidence from anecdote.
</Summary>
