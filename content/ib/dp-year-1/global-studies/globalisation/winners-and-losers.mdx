## Winners and Losers

The same forces that filled supermarket shelves with cheap goods closed the mills that once employed whole towns. Globalisation’s ledger has two columns.

<Definition term="Elephant curve">
  The **elephant curve** charts income growth by global income percentile (1988–2008): huge gains for Asia’s emerging middle class (the body) and the world’s richest (the trunk) — stagnation for Western working classes (the dip).
</Definition>

## Key points

- Winners: hundreds of millions escaped extreme poverty, mainly in Asia; consumers everywhere got cheaper goods.
- Losers: Western manufacturing workers faced wage pressure and job loss; the environment bore costs.
- The dip in the elephant curve explains much of today’s political backlash.
- Policy question: can openness be kept while compensating losers? (Retraining, safety nets.)

<Example title="Shenzhen’s rise">
  A fishing village of 30,000 in 1980, Shenzhen is now a megacity of 17 million — built by global supply chains. Extraordinary gains, alongside harsh early labour conditions.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="What does the elephant curve show?" hint="Body, dip, trunk.">
    Big income gains for emerging Asia’s middle class and the global rich; stagnation for lower-middle classes in rich countries.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Give one policy response to globalisation’s losers." hint="Compensate and retrain.">
    Retraining programmes, stronger safety nets, or wage insurance — sharing the gains with those displaced.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Globalisation cut extreme poverty dramatically.
  - Western working classes largely stagnated — the elephant’s dip.
  - Both winners and losers are real; honest accounting needs both.
  - Politics now debates how to share the gains.
</Summary>
