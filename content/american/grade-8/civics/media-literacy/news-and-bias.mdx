## News and Bias

A thousand things happened today; the news showed twelve. Choosing which twelve — and how to tell them — is where bias lives.

<Definition term="Framing">
  **Framing** is presenting an issue in a way that shapes interpretation — e.g. ‘protest’ vs ‘riot’, ‘tax relief’ vs ‘tax cuts for the rich’. Same facts, different story.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Selection bias: what gets covered (novel, dramatic, visual) vs what doesn’t.
- Framing: word choice and emphasis steer interpretation.
- Ownership and funding shape incentives — follow the money.
- Triangulate: compare multiple reputable sources, especially ones you disagree with.

<Example title="Two headlines, one protest">
  ‘Thousands march for climate action’ vs ‘Climate protesters block commuters’. Both can be true; each frames heroes and villains differently. Read both.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="What is framing? Give an example." hint="Same facts, different words.">
    Presenting facts to shape interpretation — e.g. ‘estate tax’ vs ‘death tax’ for the same policy.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why should you read sources you disagree with?" hint="Your own bubble.">
    To escape confirmation bias and selection effects — understanding how the other side frames issues sharpens your own judgement.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - News is always selected and framed.
  - Framing shapes interpretation without changing facts.
  - Ownership and incentives matter.
  - Triangulate across sources you disagree with.
</Summary>
