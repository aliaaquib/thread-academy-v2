## Levels of Government

When your streetlight breaks, you don’t call parliament. Government is layered — and knowing which layer does what is practical power.

<Definition term="Federalism">
  **Federalism** divides power constitutionally between national and regional governments (USA, Germany, India). In unitary systems (UK, France, Japan), ultimate power sits nationally, though local bodies may be granted powers.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Local: bins, planning, parks, local roads.
- Regional/state: schools, policing, transport in federal systems.
- National: defence, foreign policy, the economy, major law.
- Devolution/federalism exists because different decisions suit different scales.

<Example title="Who fixes the pothole?">
  Local council: the pothole. National government: the road-building budget and standards. Knowing the difference means complaining to the body that can actually act.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Distinguish federal and unitary systems." hint="Where does ultimate power sit?">
    Federal: power constitutionally shared between national and regional governments. Unitary: ultimate power is national; local powers are delegated.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why divide government into levels?" hint="Scale and knowledge.">
    Local knowledge and responsiveness for local issues; coordination and resources for national ones — subsidiarity in action.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Government is layered: local, regional, national.
  - Each level handles issues suited to its scale.
  - Federal systems share sovereignty; unitary ones delegate.
  - Knowing who does what makes complaints effective.
</Summary>
