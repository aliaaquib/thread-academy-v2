## Materials

A racing bike and a bridge both need strength — but one must also be light, the other cheap. Material choice is engineering judgement.

<Definition term="Composite">
  A **composite** combines two materials to get the best of both — e.g. reinforced concrete (concrete strong in compression + steel strong in tension) or carbon fibre (stiff fibres in a light resin).
</Definition>

## Key points

- Properties that matter: strength, stiffness, toughness, density, corrosion resistance, cost.
- Metals: strong and formable but heavy and corrode. Polymers: light and cheap but weaker and heat-sensitive.
- Ceramics: hard and heat-proof but brittle.
- Smart choice = right properties at acceptable cost and environmental impact.

<Example title="Why planes are aluminium (and increasingly composite)">
  Aluminium is light and strong enough; modern jets use carbon-fibre composites for wings to save fuel. Every kilogram saved is fuel saved for the plane’s whole life.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Why is glass a poor choice for a hammer head?" hint="What happens when glass is hit hard?">
    Glass is brittle — it shatters on impact instead of deforming; a hammer needs toughness.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Give one advantage of composites over single materials." hint="Best of both.">
    They combine properties — e.g. carbon fibre is as stiff as metal but far lighter.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - No perfect material exists — only trade-offs.
  - Key properties: strength, stiffness, toughness, density, cost.
  - Composites combine materials for combined strengths.
  - Choose materials by matching properties to the job.
</Summary>
