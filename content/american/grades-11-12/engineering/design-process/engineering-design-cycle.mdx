## The Engineering Design Cycle

The first aeroplanes crashed. The first light bulbs burned out in minutes. Engineering advances by cycling: each failure teaches the next design.

<Definition term="Prototype">
  A **prototype** is an early, testable version of a design — built quickly and cheaply to learn what works, not to be perfect. ‘Fail fast’ means learning from cheap failures instead of expensive ones.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Ask: define the problem and its constraints (cost, materials, safety).
- Imagine & plan: brainstorm widely, then pick the most promising idea.
- Create: build a prototype.
- Improve: test against the requirements and loop back — real design is never one-shot.

<Example title="Dyson’s 5,126 prototypes">
  James Dyson built 5,126 failed vacuum prototypes over five years before the one that worked. Each failure answered a question — that IS the design cycle.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Why do engineers build prototypes instead of going straight to the final product?" hint="What do failures cost at each stage?">
    Prototypes are cheap and fast, so failures teach lessons early — fixing a flaw in a prototype costs far less than recalling a finished product.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="A bridge design must hold 10 tonnes but cost under £1m. What are these called in the design cycle?" hint="They bound the solution.">
    Constraints (and requirements) — defined in the ‘ask’ stage.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - The design cycle: ask → imagine → plan → create → improve.
  - Iteration turns failures into information.
  - Constraints (cost, safety, materials) shape every design.
  - Prototypes exist to be tested, not to be perfect.
</Summary>
