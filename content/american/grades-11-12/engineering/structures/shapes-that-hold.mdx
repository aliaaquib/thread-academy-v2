## Shapes That Hold

An eggshell is 0.3 mm of brittle calcium carbonate — yet it survives being sat on by a hen. The secret is the dome.

<Definition term="Arch action">
  **Arch action**: a curved structure converts the downward load into compression along the curve, pushing outward at the base. Stone is weak in tension but superb in compression — so arches let stone span wide gaps.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Triangles can’t deform without changing side lengths — that’s rigidity.
- Arches turn vertical loads into compression.
- Domes are arches in 3D — enormously strong for their weight.
- Corrugation stiffens thin sheets; I-beams put material where bending stresses are highest.

<Example title="The Pantheon’s dome">
  Rome’s Pantheon (125 AD) has the world’s largest unreinforced concrete dome — 43 m across, still standing after 1,900 years. Coffers lighten it; the oculus relieves the crown.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Why does corrugated cardboard beat flat card of the same weight?" hint="Think about bending.">
    The wavy core separates the faces, giving stiffness against bending with very little material.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Where does an arch push, and why does that matter?" hint="Outward at the base.">
    An arch pushes outward at its supports (abutments), so they must be strong enough to resist the thrust or the arch spreads and collapses.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Geometry can substitute for material.
  - Triangles give rigidity; arches and domes exploit compression.
  - Put material where the stress is (I-beams, corrugation).
  - Ancient domes still stand — good shape endures.
</Summary>
