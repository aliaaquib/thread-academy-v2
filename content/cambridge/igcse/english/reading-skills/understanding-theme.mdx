## The idea beneath the story

The **plot** is what happens. The **theme** is what it *means* — the writer's big idea about life, people or society. Two stories can share a plot (a journey, a rivalry) but explore completely different themes.

<Definition term="theme">
  A **theme** is a central idea or message explored in a text — e.g. "power corrupts",
  "appearances deceive", "growing up means accepting loss". It is stated as a full idea, not a single word.
</Definition>

"Friendship" is a **topic**. "Friendship demands sacrifice" is a **theme**. Examiners reward the second — it shows thinking, not labelling.

## Finding the theme

Ask three questions of any text:

1. **What changes?** What does the main character learn, lose or gain? Change points at meaning.
2. **What repeats?** Images, words or situations that recur are rarely accidents — repetition is emphasis.
3. **What does the ending say?** Endings deliver the verdict on everything before them.

<WorkedExample title="Worked example">
  A story ends with a hero giving up a prize to save a rival. The plot: a competition.
  The change (selfish → selfless), the repeated images of hands reaching out, and the ending
  all point to one theme: **true victory is measured by character, not trophies.**
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Prove it with evidence">
  A theme statement is a claim — and claims need evidence. Always follow with a quotation
  and explain *how* it shows the theme. "The writer shows this when…" is the most useful
  sentence in English essays.
</ImportantNote>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Turn the topic 'courage' into a theme statement." hint="Make it a full idea — a claim about courage.">
    Example: "Courage means acting despite fear, not without it." Any complete, arguable idea works.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="A novel ends with two enemies planting a tree together. Suggest a theme." hint="What does the ending's image suggest?">
    Example: "Reconciliation is possible — even enemies can build something together." Focus on what the image *means*.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Theme is the big idea; plot is what happens; topic is the subject area.
  - State themes as full, arguable ideas — not single words.
  - Track change, repetition and endings to find a theme.
  - Always support theme statements with quotations and explanation.
</Summary>
