## Pollution

For most of human history, waste simply rotted away. Industrial societies produce waste faster than ecosystems can process it — and the excess accumulates.

<Definition term="Pollutant">
  A **pollutant** is a substance introduced into the environment that causes harm — to organisms, ecosystems, or human health. The dose matters: even natural substances pollute in excess.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Air pollution: particulates and gases from burning fossil fuels harm lungs and form smog.
- Water pollution: fertiliser runoff causes eutrophication — algal blooms that suffocate rivers.
- Plastic pollution persists for centuries and enters food webs as microplastics.
- Noise and light pollution disrupt animal behaviour too.

<Example title="Eutrophication in a lake">
  Fertiliser washes from fields into a lake. Nutrients feed an algal bloom that blocks sunlight; when the algae die, decomposers use up the oxygen. Fish suffocate. One nutrient, added in excess, collapses the system.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Explain how fertiliser runoff can kill fish." hint="Follow the chain: nutrients → algae → death → decomposition.">
    Nutrients cause algal blooms; dead algae are decomposed by bacteria that consume dissolved oxygen, suffocating fish.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Give one way individuals can reduce plastic pollution." hint="Think: refuse, reduce, reuse.">
    Any sensible answer: carry reusable bags/bottles, avoid single-use plastics, recycle properly.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Pollution overwhelms nature’s ability to absorb waste.
  - Air, water, soil, noise and light can all be polluted.
  - Eutrophication shows how excess nutrients collapse aquatic ecosystems.
  - Reducing pollution starts with producing less waste.
</Summary>
