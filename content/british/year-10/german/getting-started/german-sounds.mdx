## German Sounds

German spelling is wonderfully regular: learn each letter’s sound once, and it (almost) never changes. The famous difficulty isn’t pronunciation — it’s grammar.

<Definition term="Umlaut">
  An **umlaut** (ä, ö, ü) changes a vowel’s sound: ä like ‘air’, ö like ‘bird’ (lips rounded), ü like ‘few’ (lips rounded). The ß (Eszett) is simply ‘ss’.
</Definition>

## Key points

- W = English V (‘Wasser’ = VAH-ser). V = English F (‘Vater’ = FAH-ter).
- CH: throaty after a/o/u (‘Bach’); soft ‘sh’-like after e/i (‘ich’).
- EI = ‘eye’ (‘mein’); IE = ‘ee’ (‘Liebe’) — the order tells you.
- Z = ‘ts’ (‘Zeit’ = TSAIT). J = ‘y’ (‘ja’ = yah).

<Example title="Reading aloud">
  ‘Die Straße’ → DEE SHTRAH-seh. ‘Entschuldigung’ → ent-SHOOL-dee-gung. Every letter earns its place — say them all.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="How do you pronounce ‘w’ and ‘v’ in German?" hint="They swap with English.">
    W sounds like English V; V sounds like English F.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What’s the difference between ‘ei’ and ‘ie’?" hint="The second letter wins.">
    ‘ei’ sounds like ‘eye’; ‘ie’ sounds like ‘ee’.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - German spelling is highly regular.
  - W=V, V=F, Z=ts, J=y.
  - ä, ö, ü are umlauts; ß = ss.
  - EI=‘eye’, IE=‘ee’.
</Summary>
