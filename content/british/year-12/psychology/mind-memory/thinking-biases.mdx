## Thinking and Biases

Answer quickly: a bat and ball cost £1.10; the bat costs £1 more than the ball. How much is the ball? If you said 10p, your fast brain just tricked you (it’s 5p).

<Definition term="Cognitive bias">
  A **cognitive bias** is a systematic pattern of deviation from rationality — the brain’s shortcuts (heuristics) applied where they mislead, like judging likelihood by how easily examples come to mind.
</Definition>

## Key points

- System 1: fast, intuitive, emotional. System 2: slow, analytical, effortful.
- Confirmation bias: we notice evidence that agrees with us.
- Availability heuristic: vivid events feel more likely than they are.
- Anchoring: the first number you hear drags your estimate toward it.

<Example title="Fear of flying vs driving">
  Plane crashes make the news; car crashes don’t. The availability heuristic makes flying feel dangerous, though per kilometre it’s far safer than driving.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="A doctor says a test is ‘90% accurate’. Why might patients misunderstand the risk?" hint="What’s the base rate of the disease?">
    They ignore base rates: if the disease is rare, most positives are false positives — accuracy alone doesn’t give your personal probability.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="How could you counter confirmation bias in a debate?" hint="Steel-man the other side.">
    Deliberately seek disconfirming evidence; argue the opposing case as strongly as you can before deciding.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Fast thinking is efficient but biased.
  - Biases include confirmation, availability and anchoring.
  - Knowing about biases doesn’t remove them — but systems (checklists, base rates) help.
  - Good reasoning is a skill, not just intelligence.
</Summary>
