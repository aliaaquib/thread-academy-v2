## Social Class

In many countries, a child born in the poorest area can expect to die nearly a decade earlier than one born in the richest. That gap has a name: class.

<Definition term="Life chances">
  **Life chances** (Weber) are the opportunities people have to achieve valued outcomes — health, education, income, longevity. Class position systematically shapes them from birth.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Marx: class = relationship to the means of production (bourgeoisie vs proletariat).
- Weber: class + status (prestige) + party (power).
- Modern measures: occupation, income, education — and Bourdieu’s cultural/social capital.
- Class affects health, education, crime victimisation and political influence.

<Example title="The 10-year gap">
  In England, men in the most deprived areas live about 10 years less than those in the least deprived. Same country, same healthcare system — different class, different lifespan.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="How do Marx and Weber differ on class?" hint="Economics only, or more?">
    Marx: class is purely economic — ownership of the means of production. Weber: class (market position) plus status (prestige) and party (power).
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="What is cultural capital, and how does it advantage some children in school?" hint="Bourdieu: the unwritten know-how.">
    The tastes, language and confidence valued by institutions; middle-class children arrive already fluent in the school’s culture.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Class systematically shapes life chances.
  - Marx saw economic class; Weber added status and power.
  - Cultural and social capital transmit advantage invisibly.
  - Health and longevity gaps reveal class in the body.
</Summary>
