## Social Mobility

Every society claims the talented can rise. Sociologists ask: how often does it actually happen — and for whom?

<Definition term="Social mobility">
  **Social mobility** is movement between social classes — upward or downward, within a lifetime (intragenerational) or between generations (intergenerational). Absolute mobility = the whole structure shifting; relative mobility = changing position within it.
</Definition>

## Key points

- Absolute mobility rose with industrialisation — more middle-class jobs for everyone’s children.
- Relative mobility (your chances vs someone else’s) has barely budged in many countries.
- Education helps individuals but credentials inflate: degrees are worth less as more people hold them.
- The ‘glass floor’: affluent children rarely fall far, whatever their grades.

<Example title="The Great Gatsby curve">
  Countries with higher inequality have lower mobility — the rungs are further apart and harder to climb. Scandinavia is mobile; the US and UK less so than they believe.
</Example>

## Practice

<PracticeQuestions>
  <PracticeItem question="Distinguish absolute and relative mobility." hint="Bigger pie vs bigger slice.">
    Absolute: more people moving up because the class structure itself expanded. Relative: individuals changing rank within an unchanged structure.
  </PracticeItem>
  <PracticeItem question="Why hasn’t expanding education equalised mobility?" hint="Credentials inflate; advantages persist.">
    Degrees devalue as they spread, and affluent families buy advantages (tutoring, networks, unpaid internships) that qualifications alone can’t offset.
  </PracticeItem>
</PracticeQuestions>

<Summary>
  - Mobility = movement between classes, up or down.
  - Absolute mobility grew; relative mobility is sticky.
  - Education aids individuals but hasn’t equalised chances.
  - Inequality and mobility are linked (the Gatsby curve).
</Summary>
